Abstract
As synthetic images become increasingly widespread, concerns regarding their authenticity, account-
ability, and potential misuse continue to grow. Therefore, attributing the source of a synthetic image
has become an important research topic attracting increasing attention from researchers. The goal of
this paper is to provide a systematic review of synthetic image attribution, with a focus on passive
approaches, through a unified conceptual framework grounded in statistical data analysis. Within
this framework, synthetic image attribution tasks are formulated as classification or hypothesis test-
ing problems, providing the basis for a taxonomy that organizes existing methods along several
key dimensions. The proposed taxonomy is used to distinguish among different attribution scenar-
ios (identification or verification), methodological paradigms (direct attribution or reference-based
attribution), attribution evidence ( e.g., fingerprint analysis or learned representations), attribution
granularity (i.e., whether methods distinguish among architectures or specific model instances), and
the ability to operate in the presence of unknown generators. We also summarize commonly used
datasets and review the performance of mainstream methods across the various categories under a
unified comparative perspective. Finally, we discuss the current challenges, open issues, and future
research directions in this rapidly evolving field.

1 Introduction
Recent advances in deep generative modeling have
dramatically improved the realism and control-
lability of synthetic images. From early Genera-
tive Adversarial Networks (GANs) to diffusion-
based text-to-image systems such as Stable Dif-
fusion Rombach et al. (2022), Imagen Saharia
et al. (2022), and the DALL·E series Ramesh et al.
(2021, 2022); Betker et al. (2023), modern models
can produce highly photorealistic and semanti-
cally complex imagery. At the same time, the rapid
dissemination of such generative tools raises seri-
ous ethical, legal, and security concerns. Synthetic
images can be exploited for misinformation, iden-
tity impersonation, copyright infringement, and
1
other malicious purposes Rea (2023). As gener-
ative models continue to evolve and proliferate,
distinguishing between real and synthetic content
is no longer sufficient. In several applications, it
is also crucial to identify the specific model that
was used to generate a given image, enabling
accountability, intellectual property protection,
and forensic investigation.
This requirement has spurred a growing inter-
est in Synthetic Image Attribution (SIA), a foren-
sic task that seeks to infer the generative source
of a synthetic image based solely on its observable
content. Broadly speaking, attribution methods
can be divided into two categories: proactive and
post-hoc approaches. Proactive methods embed
identifiable signatures, typically via watermark-
ing, into the generated images Yu et al. (2021,
2022b); Nie et al. (2023); Tancik et al. (2020);
Lu et al. (2025). Industrial initiatives such as
Adobe Content Authenticity 1 and Google Syn-
thID2 reflect growing interest in deployable proac-
tive traceability solutions. Nevertheless, proac-
tive approaches require model-level intervention
and may suffer from quality degradation, limited
robustness, or vulnerability to removal attacks.
Complementary to proactive approaches, post-
hoc (passive) methods attempt to infer the source
model directly from intrinsic traces left in the
generated content by the model itself, without
modifying the generation pipeline. These methods
typically rely on forensic analysis of model-specific
artifacts or fingerprints and are more flexible for
use in real-world scenarios. In recent years, a
large body of work has emerged exploring passive
SIA under different settings, including closed-set
and open-set attribution, architecture-level ver-
sus model-level attribution, and direct attribution
versus reference-based attribution methodologies.
Despite the growing interest in this area, exist-
ing surveys address synthetic image attribution
only marginally as part of broader discussions on
deepfake detection or generative AI security Khoo
et al. (2022); Wang et al. (2024b); Tariang et al.
(2024); Xu et al. (2025b); Deng et al. (2025). To
the best of our knowledge, no prior work provides
a comprehensive and systematic review specif-
ically dedicated to synthetic image attribution,

particularly focusing on post-hoc forensic tech-
niques. Moreover, the literature still lacks a clear
taxonomy capable of explicitly organizing existing
methods according to their objectives, assump-
tions, and operational requirements, complicat-
ing meaningful comparisons among approaches
addressing different attribution scenarios.
In this survey, we focus exclusively on pas-
sive synthetic image attribution for fully synthetic
images. Additionally, we do not consider deep-
fake attribution methods aimed at identifying the
source of locally manipulated content, for instance
focusing on facial regions in images or videos,
where only part of the visual content is syn-
thetically altered. In particular, we assume that
each image is associated with a single generating
source. More complex scenarios involving multiple
generators, editing pipelines, or composite gen-
eration processes fall outside the scope of this
survey.
As a first and main contribution, we intro-
duce a unified conceptual framework for syn-
thetic image attribution, adopting a statistical
data analysis perspective that models source attri-
bution either as a classification problem or as
a hypothesis testing task. Building on this per-
spective, we propose a taxonomy that organizes
existing approaches along several key dimensions,
including the attribution objective ( e.g., source
identification vs. source verification), the opera-
tional setting ( e.g., closed-set vs. open-set attri-
bution), the attribution granularity ( e.g., model-
level vs. architecture-level attribution), and the
methodological paradigm ( e.g., fingerprint analy-
sis, reference-based or reconstruction-based). This
structured view clarifies the relationships among
attribution tasks, highlights commonalities and
differences among existing methods, and provides
a consistent basis for their interpretation and
comparison.
As a second contribution, we systematically
review state-of-the-art approaches through the
lens of the proposed taxonomy, grouping them
into homogeneous classes. The conceptual anal-
ysis is complemented by an empirical overview
of the performance achieved by current synthetic
image attribution methods. Existing works are
often evaluated under heterogeneous experimental
settings, using different datasets, generator selec-
tions, and evaluation metrics, making it difficult

to interpret reported results and assess the achiev-
able performance. To fill this gap, we review the
most commonly SIA datasets and evaluate repre-
sentative methods of the different categories under
common experimental conditions. The purpose of
this analysis is not to benchmarking existing solu-
tions to establish which method performs best,
but rather to provide the reader with an indica-
tion of the performance levels currently achievable
across the different attribution settings identi-
fied in our taxonomy. By considering consistent
datasets, metrics, and attribution scenarios, our
analysis provides a clear picture of the capabilities
and limitations of current passive SIA techniques,
while highlighting open challenges and promising
research directions.

survey can be summarized as follows:
• We present the first dedicated and systematic
survey of post-hoc synthetic image attribu-

• We introduce a statistical data analysis frame-

systematic organization of existing methods

clarifying their objectives, assumptions, and

• We review and position existing methods within

the goal of illustrating the performance levels
currently achievable across different attribution

2 Generative Models
Recent years have witnessed rapid advances in
generative modeling, enabling the synthesis of
highly realistic images and accelerating the pro-
liferation of AI-generated visual content. Before
reviewing synthetic image attribution methods,
we briefly outline the main families of image gen-
erative models that are commonly involved in
attribution studies.
Among the various paradigms proposed over
the years, classic probabilistic generative models,
such as Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) and nor-
malizing flows, provide tractable latent-variable
and likelihood-based formulations for image gener-
ation. Subsequently, Generative Adversarial Net-
works (GANs), autoregressive models (ARs),
and diffusion models (DMs) emerged as major
paradigms for high-fidelity image generation and
text-to-image synthesis. These model families dif-
fer substantially in their generation mechanisms
and training procedures, leading to different types
of model-dependent traces that may be exploited
for source attribution.
2.1 Variational Autoencoders and
Normalizing Flows
Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) learn a latent-
variable generative model by combining an
encoder, which maps an input image to a
latent distribution, with a decoder, which recon-
structs or generates images from latent sam-
ples Kingma and Welling (2013). Training is typ-
ically based on maximizing a variational lower
bound that balances reconstruction fidelity with
latent-space regularization. This probabilistic for-
mulation enables structured latent representations
and efficient sampling, although early VAEs often
produced blurrier images than GANs due to
their reconstruction-based objectives and likeli-
hood assumptions. Later variants, such as VQ-
VAE Van Den Oord et al. (2017), introduced
discrete latent representations, which also became
important for token-based image generation.
Normalizing flows generate images by learn-
ing an invertible transformation between a simple
latent distribution, typically a Gaussian, and the
3
data distribution. Unlike GANs, which rely on
adversarial training, or diffusion models, which
synthesize images through iterative denoising,
flow-based models define an explicit likelihood
through the change-of-variables formula. Early
models such as NICE Dinh et al. (2014) and
RealNVP Dinh et al. (2016) introduced coupling
layers to construct expressive yet tractable invert-
ible transformations. Glow Kingma and Dhariwal
(2018) further improved this paradigm through
invertible 1×1 convolutions, enabling more flexible
channel mixing and higher-quality image genera-
tion.
Although VAEs and normalizing flows are
less dominant than GANs and diffusion mod-
els in current large-scale photorealistic synthe-
sis, they remain relevant in attribution bench-
marks because they represent distinct genera-
tive mechanisms. VAEs may leave traces related
to reconstruction, latent-space regularization,
decoder design, and quantization in discrete-
latent variants. In contrast, flow-based models
may introduce artifacts associated with invert-
ible transformations, coupling layers, and chan-
nel mixing. These differences can induce source-
dependent residual patterns, spectral statistics, or
feature-space regularities, providing useful cues for
generated-image detection and source attribution.
2.2 Generative Adversarial
Networks
Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) consist
of a generator that synthesizes images and a dis-
criminator that distinguishes generated samples
from real ones, with both components trained
through an adversarial objective Goodfellow et al.
(2014). During training, the generator and dis-
criminator are optimized in opposition. As the
discriminator becomes more effective, the genera-
tor’s ability to produce realistic images improves,
eventually enabling the generator to produce out-
puts that are difficult for the discriminator to
distinguish from real images.
Early extensions of GANs, such as
CGAN Mirza and Osindero (2014) and
DCGAN Radford et al. (2015), introduced condi-
tional inputs and convolutional design principles,
laying the foundation for controllable GAN-based
synthesis. Subsequent work focused on stabilizing
adversarial training and improving distribution
matching, including spectral normalization of
the discriminator (SNGAN Miyato et al. (2018))
and explicit distribution alignment via maxi-
mum mean discrepancy (MMDGAN Bi´ nkowski
et al. (2018)). Later studies incorporated richer
representation learning mechanisms, such as
self-attention for long-range spatial dependen-
cies (SAGAN Zhang et al. (2019)), contrastive
learning for class-conditional discrimination
(ContraGAN Kang and Park (2020)), and mutual
information maximization for disentangled latent
representations (InfoMax-GAN Lee et al. (2021)).
With improved optimization and representa-
tion learning, GANs were further extended toward
conditional, high-resolution, and structure-aware
generation. StarGAN Choi et al. (2018) uni-
fied multi-domain image translation within a
single generator-discriminator framework, while
AttGAN He et al. (2019) and FSGAN Nirkin et al.
(2019) explored more fine-grained, application-
driven control. ProGAN Karras et al. (2018)
introduced progressive growing to stabilize high-
resolution synthesis, and the StyleGAN series Kar-
ras et al. (2019, 2020, 2021) redesigned the gen-
erator using an intermediate latent space and
layer-wise style modulation, enabling fine-grained
control over visual attributes. In parallel, Big-
GAN Brock et al. (2019) demonstrated the effec-
tiveness of large-scale class-conditional training,
while S3GAN Luˇ ci´ c et al. (2019) extended this
direction to a semi-supervised setting. Beyond
global conditioning, GauGAN Park et al. (2019)
introduced spatially-adaptive normalization for
structure-aware synthesis, and GALIP Tao et al.
(2023) leveraged Contrastive Language-Image
Pre-training (CLIP) Radford et al. (2021) embed-
dings to guide adversarial text-to-image genera-
tion.
Together, these architectures illustrate the
evolution of GANs from adversarial image syn-
thesis to scalable, controllable, and text-guided
generation. Along this evolution, GAN-generated
images have been shown to exhibit model-
dependent artifacts, motivating extensive research
on forensic analysis and source attribution.
2.3 Autoregressive Models
Autoregressive models generate images by fac-
torizing the joint distribution of pixels or latent
4
tokens into a sequence of conditional probabili-
ties. Image synthesis is therefore formulated as a
sequential prediction problem, where each element
is generated conditioned on previous elements
and, in text-to-image settings, on the textual
prompt. The introduction of Transformer-based
architectures Vaswani et al. (2017) substantially
improved the ability of autoregressive models to
capture long-range dependencies, making them
suitable for image generation based on discrete
visual representations.
Inspired by the success of the GPT series
in language modeling, early autoregressive image
generators treated images as ordered token
sequences. ImageGPT Chen et al. (2020) demon-
strated the feasibility of directly modeling flat-
tened image tokens. To improve scalability, Tam-
ing Transformers Esser et al. (2021) combined
CNN-based tokenization with Transformer mod-
eling, compressing images into discrete codebooks
through VQGAN. Building on this paradigm,
DALL·E Ramesh et al. (2021) employed VQ-based
image tokenization and large-scale Transform-
ers for text-to-image generation. The CogView
series Ding et al. (2021a, 2022) further improved
training stability and efficiency, while Parti Yu
et al. (2022a) scaled autoregressive text-to-image
generation using a learned image tokenizer and
large Transformer models. Related works such as
Muse Chang et al. (2023) departed from strictly
sequential decoding by adopting masked image
modeling, enabling more efficient parallel genera-
tion while retaining a token-based formulation.
More recently, GPT-4o image generation has
brought renewed attention to autoregressive image
synthesis within native multimodal systems.
Unlike recent diffusion-based image generation
systems, GPT-4o image generation is described
as an autoregressive model natively embedded
within ChatGPT and capable of using multi-
modal conversational context for image generation
and transformation 3. More broadly, GPT-4o is
described as an autoregressive omni model trained
across text, vision, and audio modalities 4. This
suggests a shift from standalone autoregressive
image generators based on discrete visual tokens
toward unified multimodal systems that integrate
3https://openai.com/index/gpt-4o-image-generation-
system-card-addendum/
4https://openai.com/index/gpt-4o-system-card/
language, visual understanding, and image gener-
ation.
Since autoregressive generation proceeds
through an ordered sequence of predictions, possi-
ble artifacts may include directional dependencies
induced by token ordering, non-uniform correla-
tions among neighboring tokens or patches, and
error propagation effects when early predictions
constrain later ones. When discrete visual tok-
enizers or codebooks are used, additional traces
may arise from quantization, codebook usage
statistics, patch boundaries, or repeated token-
transition patterns. Therefore, source attribution
for autoregressive image generators may bene-
fit from analyzing not only low-level frequency
artifacts, but also spatial dependency structures,
patch- or token-level correlations, and multimodal
alignment cues.
2.4 Diffusion Models
The theoretical foundation of diffusion models
(DMs) originates from diffusion processes in sta-
tistical physics Sohl-Dickstein et al. (2015). In
this framework, data generation is formulated as
a two-step process: a forward diffusion process
gradually corrupts data by adding noise, while
a reverse denoising process iteratively removes
noise to reconstruct high-quality samples Ho et al.
(2020). By progressively refining noisy samples,
diffusion models achieve strong visual fidelity and
diversity.
While the original formulation relies on many
stochastic denoising steps, subsequent work intro-
duced more efficient parameterizations of the
reverse process. Denoising Diffusion Implicit Mod-
els (DDIM) Song et al. (2021) reinterpret sampling
as a non-Markovian implicit trajectory, enabling
deterministic or partially stochastic generation
with significantly fewer steps while preserving the
original training objective.
To reduce the high computational cost of pixel-
space diffusion, latent diffusion models (LDMs)
were proposed Rombach et al. (2022). LDMs first
encode images into a compact latent space using a
pretrained variational autoencoder and then per-
form diffusion in this lower-dimensional represen-
tation. Combined with efficient samplers such as
DDIM, this design substantially reduces computa-
tional overhead while preserving semantic fidelity,
5
making large-scale text-to-image generation prac-
tical. Following this paradigm, diffusion-based
text-to-image systems have rapidly proliferated.
Representative examples include GLIDE Nichol
et al. (2022), VQDM Gu et al. (2022), and Sta-
ble Diffusion Rombach et al. (2022), which has
become one of the most widely adopted open
frameworks. Extensions such as SDXL Podell
et al. (2024) further scale model capacity to
improve high-resolution synthesis.
Beyond discrete-time formulations, recent
works have modeled the reverse denoising process
through stochastic differential equations, offer-
ing a continuous-time view of diffusion dynam-
ics Albergo et al. (2025). In this view, genera-
tion corresponds to integrating a learned velocity
field that transports samples between noisy and
clean states, allowing more general diffusion sched-
ules Karras et al. (2022). From an architectural
standpoint, while early diffusion models relied on
U-Net backbones, recent approaches replace con-
volutional networks with transformer-based archi-
tectures Peebles and Xie (2023). Diffusion Trans-
formers (DiTs) improve scalability and modeling
capacity, and have become an important architec-
tural direction for large-scale text-to-image gen-
eration, as exemplified by the PixArt series Chen
et al. (2024c,a,b), Stable Diffusion 3 Esser et al.
(2024), and recent Flux models BlackForestLabs
(2025a,b).
As diffusion models continue to scale, efficient
adaptation techniques, including Low-Rank Adap-
tation Hu et al. (2022) and DreamBooth Ruiz
et al. (2023), facilitate parameter-efficient fine-
tuning and personalization. Owing to their robust-
ness, scalability, and high-quality generation, dif-
fusion models currently represent the prevailing
framework for large-scale image synthesis and con-
stitute a central focus in recent studies on image
attribution and multimedia forensics.
Diffusion processes and architectures can influ-
ence the high-frequency patterns observed in syn-
thesized images. In particular, diffusion-generated
images may exhibit model-specific fingerprints
associated with the denoising trajectory, sam-
pling procedure, network architecture, and VAE
decoder. These fingerprints provide important
clues for source attribution Corvi et al. (2023).
3 Conceptual Framework and
Taxonomy for Post-hoc SIA
In this section, we introduce a statistical frame-
work for post-hoc synthetic image attribution that
provides the conceptual basis for the proposed
taxonomy.
Synthetic image attribution can be naturally
viewed as an inference problem under uncertainty,
where different attribution tasks correspond to
distinct statistical decision problems. In particu-
lar, identification-oriented attribution can be for-
mulated as a classification problem, where the goal
is to assign a query image to one among a set
of candidate sources, possibly including open-set
conditions when unknown generators may occur.
Verification-oriented attribution, instead, is more
naturally cast as a hypothesis testing problem,
where the objective is either to assess whether
an image originates from a specific source or to
determine whether two images share the same gen-
erating source. This statistical viewpoint provides
the basis for organizing existing methods accord-
ing to their attribution objective, operational
assumptions, available information, and decision
granularity, while also clarifying how different
forensic cues and fingerprints enter the attribu-
tion process through the choice of discriminative
statistics.
3.1 General Definitions
Our statistical framework starts with the defini-
tion of the source space. Let G denote the set of
all possible generative sources. Each source G ∈ G
induces a probability distribution over the gener-
ated images. Given an image x, the goal of SIA
is to infer information about the source G ∈ G
from which x originates. With a slight abuse of
notation, in the following we write x ∼ G to indi-
cate that x is generated by source G, and that it
is drawn according to the probability distribution
induced by G. Depending on the problem at hand,
the source space G may be very large, potentially
including infinitely many generators. For instance,
when each source corresponds to a specific trained
model instance, variations in initialization, train-
ing data, checkpoints, or fine-tuning may generate
a vast number of distinct elements in G.
In this formulation, G contains only synthetic
generative sources. Real images may still be (and
6
often are) included in practical benchmarks as
auxiliary negative samples or as an additional
class for joint detection-attribution evaluation.
However, in our framework they are not treated
as elements of G, since they do not correspond to
synthetic generators. This choice allows us to focus
the attribution problem on the provenance of syn-
thetic images, while leaving the treatment of real
images to the specific evaluation protocols.
Let then Gk ⊂ G denote the finite subset of
generators known to the analyst during the attri-
bution process. We denote the elements of Gk as
Gk = {G1, G2, . . . , GK}, where each Gi repre-
sents a known generative model, while generators
in G \ G k are considered unknown. Importantly,
the term known generator does not necessarily
imply full access to the generator itself. Rather,
it indicates that the generator belongs to the set
of candidate sources considered by the analyst.
Depending on the attribution scenario, different
levels of information may be available about the
generators in Gk, ranging from complete access to
the model and its parameters, to access only to
generated samples, or even to more limited forms
of side information. These different assumptions
define distinct attribution settings and have a sig-
nificant impact on the design and performance of
SIA methods. Specifically, in the following, we dis-
tinguish among white-box knowledge , where the
analyst can use internal components of the can-
didate generator, such as its weights, gradients,
or encoder-decoder modules; black-box knowledge,
where the generator cannot be inspected but
can be queried to synthesize new images; and
sample-only knowledge , where the generator can-
not be queried, and attribution relies only on
pre-collected source-specific samples, ranging from
few-shot examples to large-scale reference sets.

level attribution treats each generator as a distinct
source, so that each decision class corresponds to
a single element of G. In this setting, attribu-
tion aims to identify or verify a specific trained
model instance, and models obtained from differ-
ent checkpoints, random initializations, training

More generally, coarser attribution levels are
obtained by grouping multiple sources into sub-
sets of G. Let {A1, A2, . . .} denote a family of
attribution groups, where each Ai ⊆ G contains
generators considered equivalent under a given
attribution criterion. Attribution is then formu-
lated at the group level by assigning a test image
to one of the groups rather than to an individual
model instance.
A particularly important example is
architecture-level attribution, where each group
Ai collects generators sharing the same model
architecture. In this case, all models implement-
ing the same network topology, but differing,
for instance, in training data, initialization, or
checkpoints, belong to the same attribution class.
Since such variations may generate a very large,
and in principle infinite, number of models, the
corresponding groups are naturally defined at
the level of G rather than only through the finite
set of known sources. Although architecture-level
7
attribution is the most common coarse-grained
formulation, the same framework also accommo-
dates alternative grouping criteria. For instance,
groups may collect models trained on the same
dataset, models derived from the same base gen-
erator family, or sources sharing other relevant
design properties. In all cases, the attribution
granularity is determined by the way sources are
grouped in G and by the corresponding degree of
abstraction imposed on the decision problem.
As in model-level attribution, group-level
attribution may also induce closed-set and open-
set tasks. In this case, however, complete knowl-
edge of all the models forming a group is usually
impossible. From an operational point of view,
attribution can only rely on a finite subset of
known generators. This means that, for each
group Ai, only the corresponding known subset
Ai ∩ G k is directly observable, while additional
models belonging to the same group may remain
unknown. As a consequence, coarse-grained attri-
bution may require not only generalization across
image samples and domains, but also generaliza-
tion across unseen generators belonging to known
attribution groups.

the set of known models Gk; therefore, images
generated by any model outside Gk should be
rejected, including unknown models within known

known hypothesis space is instead defined over
known groups, such as architectures or model

to known groups should be treated as general-

ing groups, whereas only images from entirely
unknown groups should be rejected. Model-level
attribution can therefore be regarded as the
finest-grained special case, where each generator
instance defines a separate attribution hypothesis.

In the identification task, the goal is to estimate
the source in G which generated a test imagex. We
can distinguish between closed-set and open-set
source identification according to whether the test
sample can only originate from a generator in Gk
or also from an unknown generator. A summary of
the closed- set and open-set model identification
problems is given in Fig. 2.
Closed-Set Identification (CSI). Closed-set
identification assumes that the test sample has
been generated by a known source. Under this
condition, identification reduces to a multi-class
classification problem over Gk:
fCSI(x) ∈ G k. (1)
Open-Set Identification (OSI). In open-set

done by rejecting the test sample or, equivalently,
by assigning it to an “unknown” category. This
corresponds to solving a multi-class classification

fOSI(x) ∈ G k ∪ {reject}. (2)

3.4 Verification-Oriented SIA
While source identification estimates a source
within G, verification evaluates relational hypothe-
ses over the same source space. As in iden-
tification, closed-set and open-set scenarios are
determined by whether the hypotheses under test
involve only generators in Gk or may also include
sources in G \ G k. In general, verification-oriented
SIA can be formulated as two distinct hypothesis
testing problems, depending on whether the goal is
to assess the hypothesis that a test image has been
generated by a specific source, or the hypothesis
that two images originate from the same source.
A summary of the different verification-oriented
model-level attribution settings is depicted in Fig.
3. A formal definition of both cases is given in the
following sections.
3.4.1 Source Verification (SV)
In this case, the goal of the verification is to
assess whether x has been generated by a claimed
source Gclaim ∈ G k, leading to the following binary
composite hypothesis test:
(
H0 : x ∼ Gclaim,
H1 : x ̸∼ Gclaim. (3)
The corresponding hypothesis testing function
can be defined as:
fSV(x; Gclaim) ∈ {accept, reject}. (4)
While the overall formulation remains the
same in closed-set and open-set conditions, the
two cases differ based on the characterization
of the alternate hypothesis H1, depending on
whether under H1 the generating source belongs
to Gk or not. Specifically, we can define the
following two subproblems:
Closed-Set SV (CS-SV). Under H1, the test
images originate from generators in Gk \ Gclaim.
In this case, Eq. (3) defines a finite composite
hypothesis test.
Open-Set SV (OS-SV). Under H1, the test
images originate from any generator in G \ Gclaim,
in which case Eq. (3) defines a general composite
hypothesis test.
3.4.2 Same Source Verification (SSV)
A second class of verification-oriented source attri-
bution problems occurs when the analyst is given
two test images (x1, x2) generated by two sources
G′, G′′ ∈ G , respectively, and is asked to decide
whether the two images have been generated by
the same source. This formulation of the verifica-
tion problem is naturally modeled by the following
hypothesis test (hereafter referred to as Same
Source Verification, SSV, test):
(
H0 : x1 ∼ G′, x 2 ∼ G′′, G ′ = G′′
H1 : x1 ∼ G′, x 2 ∼ G′′, G ′ ̸= G′′, (5)
with a test decision function having the following
form:
fSSV(x1, x2) ∈ {accept, reject}. (6)
Even in this case, we can distinguish between
various cases according to whether G′ and/or G′′
belong to Gk or not. In particular, we can define
the following subclasses of the SSV test.
Closed-Set SSV (CS-SSV) Both G′ and G′′
belong to Gk.
9
Semi-Open-Set SSV (SOS-SSV) In this case,
one of the two sources (either G′ or G′′) belongs
to Gk, while the other generally belongs to G.
Open-Set SSV (OS-SSV) Both G′ and G′′ are
generally element of G not necessarily belonging
to Gk.
At coarser attribution granularities, the SSV
problem is reformulated by replacing source equal-
ity with class membership. In this case, the goal
is to assess whether the two images originate from
sources belonging to the same attribution class,
that is:
(
H0 : x1 ∼ G′, x 2 ∼ G′′, ∃ Ai : G′, G′′ ∈ A i
H1 : x1 ∼ G′, x 2 ∼ G′′, ∄ Ai : G′, G′′ ∈ A i.
(7)
Under this formulation, the verification prob-
lem remains binary, while source identity is
replaced by class consistency across the two sam-
ples. Note that at this level of granularity, CS-SSV
and SOS-SSV remain naturally defined depend-
ing on whether one or both sources can be related
to known attribution classes, whereas fully open-
set SSV becomes difficult to operationalize, since
unknown generators cannot be explicitly assigned
to predefined grouped classes.
We conclude by observing that across iden-
tification and verification tasks, closed-set and
open-set conditions are consistently defined with
respect to the same source space G and its known
subset Gk, although their operational effect differs.
In identification, open-set conditions enlarge the
decision space through rejection, whereas in verifi-
cation, they preserve the binary decision structure
while extending the range of sources covered by
the hypotheses.

for one task to address another task requires addi-

investigating these relationships provides a use-
ful perspective to interpret and categorize several

As shown in Fig. 4, a useful way to analyze the
relationships among attribution tasks is to stratify
them according to the level of source knowledge
available to the analyst. At the inner level, we
group all closed-set problems, namely CSI, CS-
SV, and CS-SSV, where all sources involved in
the decision belong to the known source set Gk. A
second level includes OSI, OS-SV, and SOS-SSV,
where at least one source involved in the attribu-
tion process belongs to Gk, while unknown sources
may also appear. Finally, OS-SSV forms a sep-
arate level, since it is the only setting in which
neither of the two sources generating the test sam-
ples is assumed to belong to Gk. This layered
organization reflects progressively weaker source
knowledge and provides a useful perspective to
discuss when and how a solver developed for one
attribution task may be adapted to another.
A first general observation is that a solver
designed for a given attribution problem at one
level of the hierarchy can typically be used to
address the same problem at inner levels, where
stronger source knowledge is available ( e.g., a
solver designed for OSI can also be used to
10
address the CSI problem). In this case, the origi-
nal decision mechanism remains applicable, since
the assumptions under which it was developed are
still satisfied. Nevertheless, the resulting solution
could be suboptimal, as the solver may not fully
exploit the additional source information available
at the higher level.
More interestingly, a solver designed for one
attribution problem can, at least in principle,
always be reused to address any other problem
within the same level of the hierarchy, provided
that suitable decision statistics can be derived
from its outputs. The practical complexity of such
reuse, however, varies significantly depending on
the tasks involved. To support this observation,
we discuss three representative reuse cases at
the closed-set level, while analogous extensions to
other settings are omitted for brevity.
CS-SSV → CS-SV. A first natural example con-
cerns the use of a CS-SSV solver to address the
CS-SV problem. Assume that a test image x must
be verified against a claimed source Gclaim ∈ G k,
and that at least one reference image xr ∼ Gclaim
generated by the claimed source is available.
Under this condition, source verification can be
reformulated as a same-source verification prob-
lem by applying the CS-SSV solver to the pair
(x, xr). In particular, accepting the hypothesis
that x and xr originate from the same source
corresponds to accepting the source claim, while
rejecting the same-source consistency leads to
rejecting H0 in the corresponding CS-SV test. In
this case, the reuse is straigtforward, since the
original decision statistic produced by the CS-
SSV solver can be employed without additional
transformations.
CS-SV → CSI. A CS-SV solver can be reused
to address the CSI problem by applying the ver-
ification procedure sequentially to all candidate
sources in Gk. In particular, given a test image
x, the solver is applied independently to each
source Gi ∈ G k, producing a set of verifica-
tion scores that quantify the compatibility of x
with each source hypothesis. Identification is then
obtained by selecting the source associated with
the most favorable score. Note that in this case,
reuse requires access to the quantitative outputs of
the verifier rather than to its binary decisions only,
since a simple accept/reject outcome is generally
insufficient to rank multiple candidate sources.
CSI → CS-SSV. Though less obvious, it is
also possible to reuse a CSI solver to address a
CS-SSV problem by applying the classifier inde-
pendently to the two test images x1 and x2 and
combining the resulting outputs into a suitable
same-source decision statistic. In particular, the
classifier must provide, for each input image, a
complete set of class scores over Gk, rather than
only the most likely source label. These score vec-
tors can then be jointly analyzed to assess whether
the two images are more likely to originate from
the same source or from different sources. In this
case, reuse is conceptually straightforward but
operationally involved, since a sound same-source
decision requires an additional score-combination
rule and, in general, an appropriate calibration of
the resulting test statistic.
The relationships among CS-SSV, CS-SV,
and CSI are not unidirectional. In principle,
these reuse paths can also be reversed, provided
that certain additional conditions are met, most
notably the availability of full confidence scores
or access to reference images from the involved
generators.
It is important to emphasize that the possi-
bility of reusing a solver across attribution tasks
does not imply that the corresponding problems
are equivalent. Each attribution task is character-
ized by its own statistical formulation and decision
criterion; consequently, a solver developed for one
task is not, in general, optimal when applied to
another. Reuse should therefore be interpreted as
an operational possibility rather than an indica-
tion of equivalence.
Although the above discussion is primarily
conceptual, similar forms of reuse are effectively
adopted in some methods proposed in the litera-
ture. As will be shown in the following sections,
some approaches address a target attribution
problem by operationally solving a related surro-
gate task that is easier to implement or better
established from a methodological viewpoint.

• Set configuration has different implications

of the known generators in Gk, whereas in the
open-set case, the method must also account
for sources outside the known set. This typi-
cally requires either a rejection mechanism,e.g.,
thresholding, or an explicit unknown class. For
source verification, the decision space remains
binary, but the open-set assumption broadens
the negative distribution by allowing negative
samples to come not only from other known gen-
erators but also from previously unseen ones.
For same-source verification, the decision space

the formulation. In the fully open-set case (OS-

positive and negative pairs. In the semi-open-
set case (SOS-SSV), where at least one source
belongs to Gk, the negative pair distribution
becomes substantially broader.
• Attribution granularity determines both the
level of invariance required from the extracted
source traces and the definition of unknown
sources in open-set settings. Model-level attri-
bution relies on highly specific source traces
and treats each generator instance as a sep-
arate label, so images from any model out-
side the known set should be rejected, even if
the model shares the same architecture as a
known one. By contrast, architecture-level or
group-level attribution requires more transfer-
able cues that generalize across multiple model
instances within the same group; accordingly,
unseen instances of known architectures should
be accepted, whereas models from unseen archi-
tectures should be rejected. Therefore, reported
results should be interpreted with respect to the
target granularity and the inter-source distance
induced by the dataset construction. Coarser-
grained claims further require predefined source
groups and, ideally, multiple model instances
per group to explicitly test cross-instance gen-
eralization.
• Prior knowledge about the generators directly
constrains the family of applicable methods:
when interaction with candidate generators is
possible, it can be exploited explicitly at infer-
ence time; otherwise, attribution must rely only
on traces available in the query image and on
any available source-specific samples.
In the following, we discuss the methodological
paradigms and performance metrics applicable to
the various task/setting combinations described so
far.

query image itself, typically through a source-
discriminative model learned from examples gen-
erated by the candidate sources. Reference-based
attribution methods, in contrast, infer the source
by comparing the query image with explicit

the final decision is implemented by a classifier, a
similarity score, a ranking rule, or a thresholding

from the query image or through query-reference

Direct attribution methods operate
directly on the query image and output attri-
bution scores without requiring a query-specific
comparison with external references at inference
time. In most cases, they are implemented as
classifiers, embedding models, verifiers, or open-
set classifiers trained from source-labeled images.
The learned model may exploit spatial artifacts,
frequency-domain patterns, residual signals,
hand-crafted forensic cues, learned source repre-
sentations, or other source-related regularities. In
some cases, these cues are explicitly interpreted
as generator fingerprints.
From a design perspective, direct attribution
methods are naturally compatible with closed-
set identification, where the query is assigned
to one of the known sources. They can also be
extended to open-set scenarios by coupling attri-
bution scores with a rejection mechanism. In
verification settings, the same paradigm yields
source-compatibility scores that indicate whether
the query is consistent with a claimed source.
The main challenge is that the learned repre-
sentation must satisfy the invariance properties
required by the target granularity: model-level
attribution may rely on highly specific traces,
whereas architecture-level or group-level attribu-
tion requires cues that remain stable across mul-
tiple model instances sharing the same underlying
design.
Reference-based attribution methods ,
by contrast, perform attribution by measur-
ing the compatibility between the query image
and source-associated references. These references
may be generated images from the candidate
or claimed source, or representations estimated
from such images, such as fingerprints, signa-
tures, prototypes, centroids, likelihood models,
or gallery embeddings. More generally, reference-
based methods involve two coupled design choices:
how to construct the references and in which rep-
resentation space the query-reference comparison
is performed.
The construction of references depends on
the available prior knowledge. In white-box
settings, references can be obtained through
reconstruction-based procedures, such as latent-
code recovery under a candidate or claimed gen-
erator, where the score reflects how well the
generator can reproduce or explain the query
image. In black-box settings, references can be
constructed by querying the generator, for exam-
ple, by re-synthesizing images conditioned on the
13
query content. When only sample-based knowl-
edge is available, references are estimated from
generated examples, ranging from a few samples
to larger reference sets. Once these references are
available, attribution is performed by comparing
the query with them in a suitable representation
space. This space may be defined by hand-crafted
forensic cues, residual or frequency-domain fin-
gerprints, learned fingerprint embeddings, task-
specific feature extractors, frozen representations
from pretrained vision models, or even the pixel
space.
Reference-based attribution is particularly
natural for verification-like settings, where the
core question is whether a claimed source is com-
patible with the image under analysis. It can
also be applied to identification by ranking candi-
date sources according to their compatibility with
the query. Compared with fixed direct attribution
models, reference-based methods are often easier
to extend by updating or expanding the refer-
ence set. However, their performance depends on
the quality and coverage of the references and on
whether the comparison space preserves source-
specific traces rather than image content. Their
computational cost may also increase with the
number of candidate sources or reference sam-
ples, since query-reference matching is usually
performed at inference time.
Relationship between methodological
paradigm and decision rule. It is important
to stress that the distinction between direct and
reference-based attribution should not be confused
with the form of the final decision rule. In both
paradigms, the final output may be obtained by
selecting the highest-scoring candidate, threshold-
ing a compatibility score, or applying a learned
decision function. The difference lies upstream,
in the mechanism used to compute the score.
Direct attribution methods compute source scores
from the query image alone through a learned
attribution model. Reference-based methods com-
pute source scores by comparing the query with
source-associated references or reference represen-
tations. Hence, a reference-based method may
still compare fingerprints, prototypes, or learned
embeddings; it remains reference-based if the final
score is produced through query-reference match-
ing rather than directly from the query alone.

mines which aspects of performance should be
emphasized.

Same-source verification is also a binary decision

pairs generated by the same source are treated as
positive samples, while pairs from different sources
are treated as negative samples.

In this section and the next one, we review exist-

taxonomy and methodological design space intro-
duced in the previous sections. We first focus on
identification methods, and then discuss verifica-
tion methods in Section 6.
Table 1 summarizes representative methods
for source identification. For each method, we

When assigning granularity labels, we follow
the criterion introduced in Section 4. This is
necessary because the distinction between model-

always explicit in the literature. Strictly speaking,
architecture-level attribution should group multi-

class and evaluate whether images generated by

on architecture-level traces rather than model-

in architecture or training data does not nec-

closely related instances that differ only in subtle
training-related factors.

5.1 Direct Identification
Direct identification methods attribute a query
image by extracting source-related traces from
the image itself and mapping them to a source
label or source score. Under the sample-only set-
ting, such traces are learned or estimated from
previously generated examples, without query-
ing the candidate generators at inference time.
The central methodological challenge is there-
fore to extract source-discriminative traces while
suppressing image content and other nuisance fac-
tors. In the closed-set case, the label space is
17
fixed, and direct identification methods are often
implemented by discriminative models that out-
put one score for each known source. These scores
are typically realized as logits and normalized by
a softmax layer into confidence values over the
known generators, with the source assigned to the
highest-scoring class. In the open-set case, this
closed-set decision rule must be complemented
with an additional mechanism to reject images
that do not belong to any known source. We
first review closed-set methods, and then discuss
open-set extensions. Since scalability is a major
limitation of closed-set attribution, we also discuss
few-shot and incremental variants as closed-set
methods under data-scarcity or evolving-label-
space conditions.
5.1.1 Closed-Set Identification (CSI)
Closed-set identification assumes that each test
image is generated by one of the candidate
sources known during training. Under this setting,
direct identification methods usually learn source-
discriminative representations from the query
image and map them to a predefined source
label. The main methodological question is there-
fore how to learn features that are sufficiently
source-discriminative while being robust to nui-
sance factors such as image content, dataset bias,
post-processing, and, in coarser-grained settings,
variations among different instances of the same
generative architecture.
Model-level GAN attribution
Early direct identification methods mainly focused
on learning model-specific fingerprints from GAN-
generated images. Their underlying assumption
is that images produced by the same generator
share stable artifacts, whereas images from dif-
ferent generators exhibit distinguishable traces.
These traces have been explored in different rep-
resentation domains. Pixel-domain methods train
CNN-based models to learn source-discriminative
cues directly from images or residual-like statis-
tics Yu et al. (2019); Goebel et al. (2021);
Yang et al. (2021), whereas frequency-domain
methods exploit spectral artifacts, such as Dis-
crete Cosine Transform (DCT)-based patterns
related to upsampling operations Frank et al.
(2020). More structured fingerprint representa-
tions have also been investigated. For instance,
GFD-Net Yang et al. (2021) aims to disen-
tangle content-irrelevant GAN fingerprints from
image semantics so that attribution relies more on
source-specific traces than on visual content. From
a complementary perspective, BFR-VAE Ding
et al. (2021b) analyzes GAN fingerprints as latent
distributions, showing that images generated by
different model instances can form distinguishable
fingerprint distributions and thereby providing
empirical support for model-level attribution.
These works established the feasibility of
model-level attribution, while also suggesting that
learned fingerprints may still be affected by
instance-, dataset-, or content-specific factors.
Architecture-level GAN attribution
A second line of work moves from model-level
attribution to coarser architecture-level attribu-
tion. This shift changes the required invariance:
the method should not only distinguish differ-
ent source architectures, but also ignore variations
caused by training data, initialization, loss func-
tion, or fine-tuning when these variations occur
within the same architecture. DNA-Det Yang
et al. (2022) explicitly addresses this setting
by learning globally consistent patch-wise fea-
tures and evaluating cross-seed, cross-loss, cross-
finetuning, and cross-dataset generalization. Rep-
Mix Bui et al. (2022) further improves GAN
architecture attribution through intermediate rep-
resentation mixing and a compound detection-
attribution loss, enhancing semantic generaliza-
tion and robustness to benign transformations
under a fixed set of known GAN architectures.
Zhang et al. (2025) complemented this line from
a frequency-domain perspective, using an input-
adaptive DCT mask to suppress content-related
spectral components and enhance generator fin-
gerprints, together with sub-image aggregation
and distortion augmentation for improved robust-
ness.
These works indicate that architecture-level
attribution requires a stronger form of invari-
ance than conventional closed-set model attribu-
tion, since the model must suppress instance-
specific, content-related, and post-processing-
sensitive traces while retaining architecture-
consistent cues.

Beyond-GAN attribution
More recent closed-set methods extend source
identification beyond conventional GAN-based
attribution to diffusion models, text-to-image
systems, scientific images, and mixed generative-
model families. Early studies showed that
standard supervised classifiers can capture
source-discriminative traces in diffusion-
generated images; for example, Das et al.
(2023) used a ResNet-50-based transfer-learning
model for closed-set attribution among several
diffusion/text-to-image generators and evaluated
robustness to common post-processing. Carde-
nuto et al. (2024) extended source attribution
to synthetic Western blot images, using residual
Fourier and texture-based artifacts with RF and
XGBoost classifiers to assign images to either
the pristine class or one of several GAN- and
diffusion-based source models. Subsequent works
explored richer feature spaces and multimodal
cues. DE-FAKE Sha et al. (2023) combines visual
and textual CLIP features, FIDAVL Keita et al.
(2024) adapts a vision-language model through
soft prompt tuning, and DeepGuard Namani
et al. (2025) follows a detection-then-attribution
pipeline. Xu et al. (2025a) further showed that
attribution cues in modern text-to-image diffu-
sion models may reside not only in high-frequency
artifacts, but also in style and mid-level structural
representations.
Another direction revisits low-level,
reconstruction-based, or internal generative-
model cues. Whodunit Wißmann et al. (2024)
analyzes pixel- and frequency-domain features,
including DCT, power spectral density, and auto-
correlation, for attributing diffusion-generated
images, including fine-tuned versions of Stable
Diffusion. ReTD Chen et al. (2025) uses VAE
reconstruction differences as source-related traces
and trains a Vision Transformer (ViT) Doso-
vitskiy (2020) to learn their fine-grained
distributions, whereas MAID Zhu et al. (2025)
extracts diffusion model activations through an
inverse diffusion process and trains a classifier
on these representations. ManiFPT Song et al.
(2024) provides a more principled formulation
by defining generative artifacts as deviations
from the real data manifold and fingerprints as
collections of such artifacts.
Taken together, these works show that source-
related cues beyond GAN-generated images can
emerge at multiple levels, including residual arti-
facts, low-level statistics, reconstruction differ-
ences, style and structural representations, pre-
trained feature spaces, and internal representa-
tions of generators.
Few-shot and incremental attribution
Despite their effectiveness, closed-set attribution
models do not naturally scale to a growing num-
ber of generators. Adding a new source usually
requires collecting new samples and updating the
model, which may be costly or impractical. This
limitation motivates few-shot and incremental
attribution strategies. Marra et al. (2019b) intro-
duced an incremental learning strategy to add new
GAN classes while mitigating catastrophic forget-
ting. Bindini et al. (2024) showed that lightweight
autoencoders can be trained with very few sam-
ples from each generator and extended incre-
mentally by adding new model-specific encoders.
LORAX Sullivan-Pao et al. (2025) adopts low-
rank adaptation to efficiently extend attribution
models.
These methods improve scalability under data
scarcity or evolving-label-space conditions, but
they should not be confused with strict open-set
identification. Once a few samples or prototypes
from a new generator are available and used to
update the known label space, the new genera-
tor becomes part of the candidate set rather than
remaining an unknown source to be rejected at
inference time.
5.1.2 Open-Set Identification (OSI)
Open-set identification extends closed-set identi-
fication by requiring the system to reject images
generated by sources outside the known set. Most
existing direct identification methods address this
problem through sample rejection: a model is
trained on images from known generators, and
a test image is assigned to one of the known
sources only if its confidence, or compatibility
with the known set, is sufficiently high. Other-
wise, the image is rejected as originating from
an unknown generator. This setting is more prac-
tical than approaches requiring examples from
unknown generators during training, since such
19
examples are generally unavailable in real deploy-
ments.
Static open-set attribution
Static open-set methods assume that the set of
known generators is fixed during training and
focus on rejecting unknown sources at infer-
ence time. A common strategy is classification
with rejection , inherited from open-set recogni-
tion (OSR) Scheirer et al. (2012). Starting from a
closed-set discriminative model, rejection can be
performed by thresholding confidence or uncer-
tainty scores derived from its output, such as the
maximum softmax probability (MSP) Hendrycks
and Gimpel (2016), the maximum logit score
(MLS) Hendrycks et al. (2019), or energy-based
scores Liu et al. (2020). More calibrated strate-
gies, such as OpenMax Bendale and Boult (2016),
further model the activation-space distribution
of known classes and allocate probability mass
to an explicit unknown class. In general, these
methods accept the predicted known source only
when the corresponding confidence is sufficiently
high, or equivalently, when the sample is suffi-
ciently compatible with the known-class distribu-
tion; otherwise, the image is rejected as unknown.
Their effectiveness, therefore, depends on whether
the learned representation forms compact and
well-separated regions for known sources while
preventing unknown-source samples from being
over-confidently absorbed into the known space.
Several works adapt this paradigm to synthetic
image attribution. Wang et al. (2023a) proposed
a ViT-based hybrid architecture for open-set clas-
sification of GAN-based image manipulations and
source attribution, where rejection is performed
by analyzing classifier outputs with MSP, MLS, or
OpenMax. Cioni et al. (2024) showed that inter-
mediate features extracted from large pretrained
vision models, such as CLIP Radford et al. (2021)
and DINOv2 Oquab et al. (2024), provide effec-
tive representations for open-set origin attribution
beyond GAN-only settings. In their linear-probing
variant, the extracted query features are fed into
a logistic regressor, and unknown sources are
rejected according to the classifier’s confidence.
A second group of methods explicitly mod-
ifies the training procedure to reduce open-
space risk. Yang et al. (2023) introduced POSE,
which progressively simulates the open space of
unknown models through lightweight augmenta-
tion models. These augmentation models preserve
image semantics while modifying model-specific
traces, thereby providing synthetic open-set sam-
ples around the boundary of known sources. Wang
et al. (2024a) studied the role of mixup aug-
mentation for synthetic image attribution in the
wild. Their analysis shows that standard mixup
improves robustness under dataset mismatch, but
may enlarge the known-source region and degrade
open-set rejection. To alleviate this trade-off,
they proposed frozen-label mixup, which preserves
most of the robustness benefit while mitigating
the loss in open-set performance. Along a different
direction, Wang et al. (2025) proposed BOSC, a
backdoor-based open-set classification framework.
During training, class-specific triggers are injected
into a subset of images so that the network
learns a controlled association between known-
source features and trigger responses. At inference
time, unknown samples are rejected according to
their response to these triggers, yielding a dedi-
cated rejection score rather than relying only on
standard classifier confidence.
A further line of learned open-set methods
improves generalization by learning forensic rep-
resentations without using images from real tar-
get generators. Fei et al. (2026) proposed OFA,
a synthesis-free framework that first trains a
stochastic reconstruction encoder-decoder on real
images as a fingerprint simulator. Different sam-
pled decoder architectures serve as simulated
source models, producing reconstructed images
with diverse model-like fingerprints. With the
simulator frozen, OFA trains a fingerprint extrac-
tor on the amplitude-spectrum residuals of these
reconstructed images using supervised contrastive
learning. For open-set identification, OFA adds a
rejection-aware classification head on top of the
learned fingerprint extractor, using reconstructed
images from held-out simulated architectures to
supervise the rejection class.
These methods aim to learn compact and
discriminative representations for in-set sources,
thereby reducing the open-space risk, which refers
to the risk of assigning high confidence to sam-
ples lying far from the known training distribu-
tion Jiang et al. (2023).

Dynamic open-set attribution
The methods discussed above are static: the set
of known generators is fixed during training, and
unknown sources are only rejected at inference
time. A different family of methods considers
dynamic scenarios, where unknown sources may
be discovered, clustered, or incorporated into the
known set over time.
Girish et al. (2021) addressed GAN attri-
bution in a discovery-oriented setting. Besides
labeled images from known sources, their method
assumes access to an unlabeled set that may con-
tain images from unknown GANs. The framework
iteratively combines representation learning, out-
of-distribution detection, clustering, merging, and
refinement to group unknown images into emerg-
ing source clusters. This differs from standard
OSI, where unknown-source samples are not avail-
able during training, and the system only needs
to reject them at inference time. Instead, Girish
et al. discover unknown sources from an unlabeled
set and update the attribution model through
iterative retraining.
A further dynamic setting arises when the
known source set evolves. Qin et al. (2026) pro-
posed IncreFA, which reformulates attribution as
an incremental open-set learning problem. New
generators are progressively added as new classes,
while old knowledge is preserved through a latent
memory bank. The method also exploits hierarchi-
cal relationships among generators, such as GAN,
diffusion, and autoregressive families, to encour-
age separation at the family level while retaining
model-level discrimination. Open-set awareness is
introduced by mixing latent features from dif-
ferent known classes to synthesize pseudo-unseen
samples, and inference-time rejection is performed
by thresholding the MSP over known generators.
Thus, despite its dynamic and hierarchical design,
IncreFA remains a classification-with-rejection
method extended with continual learning, latent
replay, and pseudo-unseen calibration.
5.2 Reference-based Identification
Building on the reference-based paradigm intro-
duced in Section 4.1, reference-based identification
attributes a query image by ranking candidate
sources according to compatibility scores. Each
score is obtained by comparing the query with
one or more reference samples from the candidate
source, or with source representations estimated
from such samples. In closed-set identification, the
query is assigned to the most compatible source; in
open-set identification, this assignment is accepted
only if the best score exceeds a rejection thresh-
old, otherwise the query is rejected as originating
from an unknown source.
This source-wise scoring formulation corre-
sponds to the SV-to-identification reuse paths
discussed in Section 3.5: CS-SV can solve CSI
by scoring all candidates and selecting the most
compatible source, while OS-SV can solve OSI
by adding a rejection threshold. The meth-
ods reviewed below mainly differ in how they
instantiate the compatibility score under differ-
ent knowledge settings, including reconstruction
scores, resynthesis similarities, source-wise like-
lihoods estimated from reference samples, and
distances to fingerprints, prototypes, centroids, or
gallery representations.
5.2.1 White-box prior knowledge.
White-box reference-based methods assume access
to the candidate generators, including their archi-
tectures and parameters, and attribute a query
image by identifying which generator can best
reconstruct or explain it. Albright et al. (2019) for-
mulated this problem through generator inversion:
for each candidate generator, the latent vector is
optimized so that the generated image approxi-
mates the probe image, and attribution is assigned
to the generator with the smallest reconstruc-
tion error. Later inversion-based methods follow
the same principle, but differ in the optimization
objective and in the space where reconstruction
error is measured, ranging from pixel-space recon-
struction Albright et al. (2019) to feature-space
or perceptual losses Zhang et al. (2020); Hirofumi
et al. (2022). White-box inversion provides explicit
source-specific reconstruction cues through the
recovered latent code and reconstructed image,
but its applicability is limited by the need for
white-box access, the cost, and instability of per-
generator optimization.
5.2.2 Black-box prior knowledge.
In the black-box setting, the candidate generators
can be queried, but their parameters and inter-
nal representations are unavailable. Bongini et al.
(2025b) exploited this form of access through a
21
training-free resynthesis strategy. Given a query
image, the method first obtains a textual descrip-
tion of its content using an image-to-text model.
This description is then used as a prompt to query
each candidate text-to-image generator, produc-
ing query-conditioned reference images. Attribu-
tion is performed by selecting the generator whose
resynthesized images are closest to the query
in a pretrained feature space, such as CLIP
or DINOv2. This strategy is naturally scalable
to newly added black-box generators, since no
source-specific discriminative model needs to be
retrained. Its effectiveness, however, depends on
the quality of the recovered description, the cost of
querying candidate generators, and the ability of
the feature space to preserve source-related traces
while discounting semantic variation.
5.2.3 Sample-only prior knowledge.
When only labeled image samples are available,
without access to the candidate generators them-
selves, source-specific representations must be
estimated from those samples. Early works in this
direction were inspired by the analogy between
GAN fingerprints and camera Photo-Response
Non-Uniformity (PRNU) Lukas et al. (2006).
Marra et al. (2019a) showed that images produced
by the same GAN share a stable residual pat-
tern. They estimate this fingerprint by subtracting
a denoised image from each generated image,
averaging the resulting residuals, and perform-
ing source attribution through residual-fingerprint
correlation. This line of work established that
source-specific traces can be explicitly represented
as sample-derived fingerprints rather than learned
only through a discriminative decision boundary.
Other methods learn a feature space wherein
reference matching is more effective. Xuan et al.
(2019) used Steganalysis Rich Model (SRM) fil-
tering Fridrich and Kodovsky (2012) followed
by a ResNet-based metric-learning framework
to suppress semantic content and learn source-
discriminative embeddings. At inference time, the
query embedding is compared with a template
image library, and the nearest template deter-
mines the predicted source. A similar sample-only
matching rule is adopted by the nearest-neighbor
variant of Cioni et al. (2024). It extracts inter-
mediate CLIP or DINOv2 features for both the
query and the labeled training images, applies
ℓ2 normalization, and performs cosine-similarity
matching over the training gallery. Source pre-
diction is obtained from similarity-weighted top-k
neighbor votes, while open-set rejection uses the
maximum normalized kNN class score.
Reference-based designs naturally extend to
open-set identification by combining source-wise
scoring with a rejection rule based on distance or
likelihood. Fang et al. (2023) proposed a metric-
learning approach in which each known generator
is represented by a reference point in the learned
embedding space. A query is first assigned to the
nearest candidate source and is then accepted
or rejected according to its normalized distance
from the corresponding reference point. Li et al.
(2024) further explored reference-based finger-
print classification for both GAN and diffusion
sources. Their method combines handcrafted high-
pass filters, learnable filters, and CLIP seman-
tic features to obtain compact fingerprints. The
test fingerprint is compared with reference fin-
gerprints from known classes, and the sample is
assigned to the nearest source only if its dis-
tance falls below a threshold; otherwise, it is
rejected as unknown. For rejected samples, the
method further performs coarse attribution to
the closest generator family, such as GAN or
diffusion, using family-level centers. Likelihood-
based source modeling provides another way to
instantiate the same principle. Cardenuto et al.
(2024) studied source attribution for synthetic
Western blot images using explainable residual
artifacts. For each pristine or synthetic source,
they trained a one-class classifier on residual
Fourier and texture-based features, and assigned
the query to the source with the highest likeli-
hood. The assignment can be rejected as unknown
when the maximum likelihood falls below a thresh-
old, yielding a source-wise likelihood-based open-
set attribution scheme. Similarly, Nguyen et al.

5.3 Discussion
Overall, identification remains the dominant for-
mulation in synthetic image source attribution,
largely because it directly matches the multiclass
setting used in many benchmark datasets. Exist-
ing methods have evolved from closed-set GAN
attribution toward broader settings involving
architecture-level generalization, open-set rejec-
tion, few-shot adaptation, and source-wise ref-
erence matching. The main limitation is scal-
ability: fixed-label discriminative models must
be updated when new sources appear, while
reference-based strategies reduce this burden but
depend on the availability, diversity, and repre-
sentativeness of source references. These issues
motivate verification-oriented formulations, where
the decision is made with respect to a claimed
source rather than a candidate set.
6 Attribution as Verification
Table 2 summarizes methods that formulate syn-
thetic image attribution as a verification problem.
A first observation is that verification-oriented
methods are much fewer than identification-
oriented ones, suggesting that existing research
has so far been dominated by multiclass identifica-
tion settings. In contrast to identification, where a
query image is assigned to one source among mul-
tiple candidates, verification does not necessarily
require a closed multiclass decision space. Instead,
it asks whether a query image is compatible with
a claimed source, or whether two images originate
from the same source. Accordingly, the decision is
usually binary or pairwise.
Although verification includes both source ver-
ification (SV) and same-source verification (SSV),
this section mainly reviews methods whose final
decision supports or rejects a source claim. SSV
is discussed when it is explicitly addressed as
an independent task, or when it is used opera-
tionally to support SV by comparing the query
with reference samples or reference representa-
tions associated with the claimed source. This
organization is consistent with the task-reuse rela-
tionships discussed in Section 3.5.
Following the methodological distinction intro-
duced in Section 4.1, we also group verification
methods into direct verification and reference-
based verification approaches. Direct verification
23
methods accept or reject a source claim by analyz-
ing source-related traces extracted from the query
image itself. Reference-based verification methods
instead compute a compatibility score between the
query image and claimed-source references, such
as reference images, source fingerprints or signa-
tures estimated from reference samples, query-
conditioned reference images, or query-specific
reconstructions under the claimed generator. In
Table 2, the available knowledge is defined with
respect to the claimed generator or claimed source.
As in Section 5, the granularity labels follow the
criterion introduced in Section 4.
6.1 Direct Verification
Direct verification methods determine whether a
query image is consistent with a claimed source
by extracting source-related traces and mapping
them to an accept/reject decision. This can be
implemented through binary classification, one-
class classification, or anomaly detection. Unlike
identification, the output is not a label among
multiple candidate sources, but a binary decision
that can be used to support or reject a source
claim.
Liu et al. (2024) proposed OCC-CLIP for
source verification in a few-shot sample-only set-
ting, where only a small number of images from
the claimed source are available, and the gen-
erator itself cannot be accessed. Although the
problem is formulated as a few-shot one-class
classification task, the actual training procedure
uses both target images from the claimed source
and auxiliary open-domain images as non-target
samples. OCC-CLIP optimizes learnable CLIP
prompts for the target and non-target classes, and
introduces adversarial data augmentation on the
auxiliary non-target images to better approximate
the boundary around the target distribution. This
setting is practical for closed-source or commer-
cial generators, but adding a new claimed source
generally requires constructing a new verifier.
Laszkiewicz et al. (2024) proposed FLI-
PAD, which treats source verification as an
anomaly detection problem. The method first
extracts model-dependent features through final-
layer inversion, where the inversion problem is
reduced to a convex Lasso optimization. An
anomaly detector is then trained to distinguish
samples generated by the target model from sam-
ples outside the target distribution. The method
is computationally efficient compared with full
latent optimization, but requires white-box access
to the claimed model and relies on architectural
assumptions needed for final-layer inversion.
6.2 Reference-based Verification
Reference-based verification methods evaluate the
compatibility between a query image and refer-
ences associated with a claimed source. In most
cases, these references consist of one or more
images generated by the claimed source, or source
representations estimated from such images, such
as fingerprints, signatures, prototypes, or pair-
wise matching scores. When the claimed generator
can be queried or accessed, the reference can
also be constructed in a query-specific manner,
either by generating semantically aligned images
or by reconstructing the query under the claimed
model. Following the organization adopted for
reference-based identification in Section 5.2, we
group these methods according to the available
prior knowledge about the claimed source, mov-
ing from white-box to black-box and sample-only
settings.
6.2.1 White-box prior knowledge
White-box reference-based verification methods
assume access to the claimed generator, or to a
relevant component of it, and construct query-
specific references or scores through inversion,
reconstruction, or reconstruction statistics under
the claimed model. The verification score then
measures how well the claimed model can explain
the query image.
Hirofumi et al. (2022) formulated post-hoc
GAN verification through latent recovery. Given
a claimed GAN and a suspicious image, the
latent code is optimized so that the claimed
generator reconstructs the query as faithfully as
possible. The resulting reconstruction acts as a
query-specific reference, and the source claim is
accepted if the reconstruction error is sufficiently
small. This method is computationally expensive
because latent optimization must be performed for
each query.
RONAN Wang et al. (2023b) generalizes
this idea to different types of generative mod-
els by reverse-engineering the model input. For
24
a claimed model, the optimized reconstruction
provides the query-specific reference, while the
calibrated reconstruction loss serves as the veri-
fication score. To reduce image-complexity bias,
RONAN estimates the reconstruction-loss distri-
bution on images generated by the claimed model,
calibrates the query loss using a reference gen-
erative model, and applies statistical hypothesis
testing. Since RONAN performs input reverse-
engineering on the inspected generative model
rather than only on an autoencoding component,
its access assumption is stronger, but its attribu-
tion target is correspondingly closer to the full
generative model.
LatentTracer Wang et al. (2024c) adapts
reconstruction-based verification to latent gen-
erative models. Instead of inverting the full
text-to-image generation process, it focuses on
the decoder of the inspected latent generative
model and initializes latent optimization with the
encoder projection of the query image. This pro-
duces a query-specific reconstruction whose error
is used as the attribution signal. By exploiting
the invertibility of the autoencoder, LatentTracer
improves the efficiency and separability of latent
inversion, but its score is mainly tied to traces
introduced by the decoder.
AEDR Wang et al. (2026) further replaces
optimization-based reconstruction with autoen-
coder double-reconstruction. It performs two con-
secutive reconstructions through the autoencoder
associated with the claimed model and uses the
ratio between the corresponding reconstruction
losses as the attribution signal. Here, the claimed-
source reference is not a single reconstructed
image, but the reconstruction behavior of the
claimed autoencoder. The intuition is that images
generated by the claimed source already lie close
to the corresponding autoencoder manifold and
therefore exhibit stable losses across consecu-
tive reconstructions, whereas images from other
sources are first projected toward that manifold
and show a larger loss reduction. The ratio is fur-
ther calibrated by an image homogeneity measure
to reduce image-complexity bias. Since AEDR
only queries the autoencoder rather than the full
generative pipeline, it is efficient and training-free,
but its access assumption and attribution signal
are explicitly component-level.
For autoencoder- or decoder-based methods
such as LatentTracer and AEDR, the result-
ing attribution should therefore be interpreted
with care. Although these methods are formu-
lated as model-level verification, they mainly test
compatibility with the autoencoding component
associated with the claimed model. When mul-
tiple latent generative models share the same
or highly similar autoencoder/decoder, the effec-
tive granularity may degrade from full model-level
verification to autoencoder- or decoder-level veri-
fication.
6.2.2 Black-box prior knowledge
In the black-box setting, the claimed generator
can be queried, but its parameters and internal
representations are unavailable. Reference-based
verification methods in this setting construct
query-conditioned references by using semantic
information extracted from the query image to
generate aligned images from the claimed text-to-
image generator. The resulting verification prob-
lem is then operationally reduced to a pairwise
comparison between the query and the generated
reference image.
Jiang and Tian (2024) addressed source attri-
bution for text-to-image diffusion models by con-
structing prompt-aligned image pairs. Given a
query image, a semantic description is obtained
manually or through an image-to-text model and
then fed into the claimed text-to-image model
to generate a paired reference image. The query
and the generated reference image are processed
by a Siamese feature-difference model, which pre-
dicts whether they were generated by the same
source. Therefore, although the final goal is to
verify a claimed source, the operational task is
same-source verification over semantically aligned
query-reference pairs.
SemGIR Yu et al. (2024) follows a related
SV-via-SSV strategy. It first extracts a semantic
description from the query image and regener-
ates a semantically aligned reference image using
the claimed text-to-image model. For attribution,
SemGIR introduces semantic-conditional similar-
ity features, using the extracted text as a condition
so that the decision model focuses less on prompt-
induced semantic content and more on model-
intrinsic characteristics. The final decision is made
25
by comparing the query with the generated ref-
erence image and deciding whether the query is
compatible with the claimed model. Thus, Sem-
GIR is reference-based, because the source claim is
verified through comparison with a claimed-source
reference image, even though the compatibility
function itself is learned.
6.2.3 Sample-only prior knowledge.
When only generated samples from the claimed
source are available, reference-based verification
methods estimate source-specific representations
from those samples or compare the query with
a support set of reference images. These rep-
resentations may take the form of aggregated
frequency signatures, learned fingerprints, gallery
embeddings, or pairwise matching scores.
Joslin and Hao (2020) proposed a frequency-
domain SV method for GAN-generated images.
Their approach estimates a model-level signature
by averaging Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)-based
frequency-domain representations from images
generated by the claimed GAN, and then mea-
sures the similarity between the query image and
this signature. The method requires only gener-
ated samples from the claimed source and does not
require training a source-specific discriminative
model.
A more explicit realization of the SSV-to-SV
reuse principle is provided by pairwise reference-
based methods. Abady et al. (2024) proposed a
Siamese verification framework for open-set archi-
tecture attribution. The method considers two
scenarios: SSV, where the system decides whether
two synthetic images were generated by the same
architecture, and SV, where a query image is
compared with one or multiple reference images
from the claimed architecture. In the latter case,
source verification is obtained by applying the
same pairwise verifier to query-reference pairs and
fusing the resulting scores. Although the compat-
ibility function is learned by a Siamese network,
the method remains reference-based because the
claim is verified through comparison with refer-
ence images from the claimed source.
Fei et al. (2026) proposed OFA, a synthesis-
free framework for open-set source verification.
OFA trains a stochastic reconstruction encoder-
decoder on real images as a fingerprint simulator,
and then uses the frozen simulator to generate
reconstructed real images with diverse model-like
fingerprints. A fingerprint extractor is learned
from the amplitude-spectrum residuals of these
reconstructed images via supervised contrastive
learning, with an attribution head trained for pair-
wise same-source prediction. At inference time, a
query image is compared with a support set from
the claimed target model, and the pairwise pre-
dictions are averaged into an attribution score.
Therefore, OFA verifies a claimed source through
reference-based pairwise fingerprint matching and
can handle previously unseen target models with-
out retraining, provided that reference images
from the claimed source are available.
A related synthesis-based fingerprint-learning
strategy is adopted by Yang et al. (2025), which
focuses on same-source verification. They synthe-
size a large number of shallow generative models
by varying basic architectural components, such
as upsampling, convolution, activation, and nor-
malization layers, to mimic the frequency-domain
fingerprint patterns of real-world generators. A
fingerprint extractor is then trained on these
synthetic models and transferred to unseen real
generators. In their verification setting, two gener-
ated images are compared by the cosine similarity
between their fingerprint embeddings to decide
whether they originate from the same model.
6.3 Discussion
Overall, verification remains less explored than
identification, but it provides a complementary
formulation for claim-driven scenarios such as
ownership verification, misuse investigation, and
source-consistency checking. By avoiding assign-
ment over all possible sources, verification simpli-
fies the decision space, but introduces challenges
in verifier calibration, reference-set representative-
ness, access requirements, and effective attribution
granularity. Direct verification methods allow effi-
cient test-time verification but are often tied to a
particular claimed source. In contrast, reference-
based verification methods offer more flexible
claim evaluation but depend on the quality of the
claimed-source references and the reliability of the
compatibility score.

and evaluated. Generator-family coverage, source-
label definition, content diversity, available source
information, and training-testing source separa-
tion jointly determine whether a dataset can
support source identification, source verification,
same-source verification, open-set evaluation, or
granularity analysis. This section reviews repre-
sentative datasets proposed for, or later adapted
to, synthetic image attribution.

7.1 Dataset Composition
Existing datasets differ substantially in visual con-
tent, scale, generator coverage, and real-image
sources. In terms of content, some datasets
focus on a specific domain, such as faces Xuan
et al. (2019); Abady et al. (2024), while oth-
ers cover broader or mixed visual content, as in
OSMA Yang et al. (2023) and IABench Qin et al.
(2026). Beyond natural images, Mandelli et al.
(2022) covers real and synthetic Western blot
images. Such domain diversity is important for
evaluating whether attribution methods capture
source-specific cues rather than semantic, domain,
or dataset-specific shortcuts.
Dataset scales range from tens of thousands
of images Namani et al. (2025); Mandelli et al.
(2022), to millions of samples Bui et al. (2022);
Zhu et al. (2023). Large datasets support deep
model training and broad benchmarking, whereas
smaller datasets are often used for controlled anal-
ysis, few-shot attribution, verification with limited
target-source samples, or domain-specific forensic
studies.
Generator coverage has also evolved. Early
datasets are dominated by GAN-generated
27
images, reflecting the initial focus on GAN fin-
gerprints and source attribution. Recent datasets
increasingly include diffusion, autoregressive, and
commercial text-to-image generators. For exam-
ple, Liu et al. (2024); Abady et al. (2024); Qin
et al. (2026) include diffusion-based generators,
enabling evaluation beyond GAN-only settings.
Some datasets further consider more realistic con-
ditions, such as post-processing robustness in
WILD Bongini et al. (2025a).
Real images are commonly included either as
an additional class or as source material for syn-
thesis, with frequent sources including CelebA Liu
et al. (2015), LSUN Yu et al. (2015), Ima-
geNet Deng et al. (2009), and MSCOCO Lin
et al. (2014). Other datasets rely on more special-
ized sources; for instance, the real Western blot
images in Mandelli et al. (2022) were collected
from the web and scientific publications. Since
real-image sources often differ in content, acqui-
sition pipeline, compression, and preprocessing,
they must be carefully controlled; otherwise, attri-
bution models may exploit dataset artifacts rather
than generator-specific traces
Low-level image properties, such as resolu-
tion, file format, actual encoding, and compression
quality, may also affect attribution performance.
These properties are not necessarily spurious:
when they are inherent to the generation or
distribution pipeline of a source, they may pro-
vide useful provenance cues in practical scenarios.
However, when such differences mainly arise from
dataset construction, collection, or preprocess-
ing, their correlation with source labels may lead
attribution models to exploit dataset-specific arti-
facts rather than generator-specific fingerprints.
Therefore, these properties should be considered
when constructing or reusing attribution datasets
and documented whenever such information is
available.
7.2 Task and Protocol Flexibility
A dataset is not inherently tied to a single
SIA task. Whether it supports identification or
verification, and closed- or open-set evaluation,
depends on the protocol imposed on its source
labels and splits. Some datasets were explicitly
designed for attribution, such as OSMA Yang
et al. (2023), whereas others were originally intro-
duced for related tasks and later repurposed. For
example, GenImage Zhu et al. (2023) was pro-
posed for synthetic image detection but has been
adopted by recent attribution methods Cioni et al.
(2024); Zhu et al. (2025); Chen et al. (2025).
Similarly, Mandelli et al. (2022) originally stud-
ied real-versus-synthetic Western blot detection,
while Cardenuto et al. (2024) later used the same
source labels for synthetic Western blot attribu-
tion.
Verification-oriented datasets Liu et al. (2024);
Abady et al. (2024) can also be reorganized for
identification, and IABench Qin et al. (2026) can
support either static or incremental protocols.
However, such reuse requires explicit reporting of
training and testing source splits, source informa-
tion available at inference time, and decision rules
for fair comparison.
7.3 Granularity Support
Another important issue is whether a dataset
can support rigorous claims about attribution
granularity. The label granularity reported in
Table 3 describes the available source labels, but
it does not necessarily imply that the dataset
was designed to validate that level of attribu-
tion. This distinction is especially important for
datasets originally proposed for detection, such as
Mandelli et al. (2022) and GenImage Zhu et al.
(2023), where generator labels enable attribution
experiments only after an appropriate attribution
protocol is defined.
Architecture-level attribution should test
whether a method captures traces shared across
independently trained instances of the same archi-
tecture, rather than fingerprints tied to a spe-
cific checkpoint, seed, dataset, or implementation.
Conversely, fine-grained model-level attribution
requires controlled groups of closely related mod-
els, where the architecture is fixed and only
model-specific factors vary.
For architecture-level attribution, existing
datasets only partially meet the requirements.
Yang et al. (2022) provides one of the strongest
architecture-level benchmarks within the GAN
family through cross-seed, cross-loss, cross-
finetuning, and cross-dataset tests, but remains
GAN-centric. Attribution88 Bui et al. (2022)
supports semantic generalization analysis with
architecture-level GAN labels, but does not sys-
tematically vary training configurations within
28
each architecture. Abady et al. (2024) extends
architecture-level evaluation to GANs, diffusion
models, and transformers, but is mainly restricted
to synthetic face images and lacks large-scale
controlled instances for each architecture.
For model-level attribution, OSMA Yang et al.
(2023) partially addresses instance-level varia-
tion through its unseen-seed setting, but this
setting primarily targets unknown-source detec-
tion rather than closed-set discrimination among
closely related instances. IABench Qin et al.
(2026) includes closely related released model
versions, especially diffusion and text-to-image
models, but remains organized by released model
identity rather than controlled variations of archi-
tecture, checkpoint, seed, training data, or fine-
tuning procedure.
Therefore, current datasets can evaluate attri-
bution over their provided source labels, but
they are not always sufficient to determine
whether learned cues correspond to architecture-
level traces or fine-grained model-level finger-
prints. A rigorous granularity benchmark would
need to jointly control semantic domains, genera-
tor families, and model-instance variations.
7.4 Toward Standardized
Benchmarks
Despite the availability of several SIA datasets,
the field still lacks a standardized benchmark with
unified task definitions, data splits, representa-
tive baselines, training protocols, and metrics. A
dataset provides image samples and source labels,
whereas a benchmark further specifies how these
data are split, which tasks are evaluated, which
methods are compared, and which metrics are
used. This distinction is important because results
obtained on the same dataset may reflect different
task assumptions or evaluation protocols.
Recent studies have begun to explore
benchmark-oriented evaluation by repurposing
existing AIGC datasets. For example, Mazzola et
al. Mazzola et al. (2025) used AGIQA-3K, orig-
inally designed for AI-generated image quality
assessment, to evaluate standard CNN back-
bones for closed-set multiclass attribution. This
work provides a useful lightweight baseline and
shows that generic classifiers can distinguish
coarse generator categories more reliably than
fine-grained generator variants. It also highlights
the influence of visual consistency and class-level
similarity on attribution performance. However,
since AGIQA-3K was not specifically constructed
for attribution, and the evaluation is limited to
generic CNNs under closed-set classification, it
does not cover broader SIA settings such as open-
set attribution, source verification, robustness to
post-processing, or cross-dataset generalization.
These observations highlight the need for more
comprehensive benchmarks that can evaluate
attribution methods under unified and task-aware
protocols.
7.5 Discussion
Overall, current SIA datasets have evolved from
controlled GAN-centric settings toward more
diverse attribution scenarios involving diffusion
models, commercial generators, verification pro-
tocols, incremental learning, robustness analysis,
and domain-specific scientific images. However,
they remain heterogeneous in source granular-
ity, generator-family coverage, content domain,
preprocessing pipeline, and evaluation protocol.
Moreover, several datasets used for SIA were not
originally designed as attribution datasets, but
were adapted from detection, quality-assessment,
or forensic-analysis datasets once generator-level
labels became available. Therefore, benchmark
results should be interpreted together with dataset
assumptions and protocol biases, especially under
realistic conditions involving emerging genera-
tors, limited source information, post-processing,
domain shifts, and heterogeneous real-image
sources.
8 Experimental Analysis of
Achievable Performance
Building on the preceding review, this section
provides an empirical view of the performance cur-
rently achievable by selected representative meth-
ods for synthetic image attribution. Our goal is
not to establish an exhaustive benchmark or rank
methods in absolute terms, but to examine how
different attribution strategies behave under con-
trolled experimental settings and to identify the
limitations that remain. Since existing methods
differ in their assumptions, supervision require-
ments, source knowledge, and target tasks, the
results should be interpreted as a task-oriented
29
snapshot of current achievable performance rather
than as a universal comparison across all possible
operating conditions.
Unless otherwise specified, we follow the
default settings of each method and retrain the
corresponding models from scratch when train-
ing is required. All experiments are conducted on
an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU. We first introduce
the evaluation metrics, datasets, and experimen-
tal protocols used in our analysis, then we discuss
the results for identification (Section 8.3) and
verification (Section 8.4).
8.1 Reported metrics
The formal definitions of the evaluation metrics
used for the different attribution tasks have been
introduced in Section 4.2. Here, we only specify
the metrics reported in our experiments.
For closed-set source identification, we report
ACC. For open-set source identification, we report
AUROC and OSCR, which assess, respectively,
the discrimination between known and unknown
synthetic sources and the trade-off between cor-
rect attribution of known synthetic samples and
rejection of unknown synthetic samples.
For source verification, we report AUROC as
the main threshold-independent metric. When a
single operating point is needed, we also report
ACC. For each claimed generator Gclaim, AUROC
and ACC are computed on binary verification
sets where images generated by Gclaim are treated
as positive samples and images from the evalu-
ated source subset are treated as negative samples
when the evaluated source differs from Gclaim.
Therefore, each source-specific value measures the
verification performance between Gclaim and the
evaluated source subset. We refer to the case
where the evaluated source is identical toGclaim as
the matched-source case. In this case, AUROC is
not defined because no negative samples are avail-
able; for ACC, the corresponding value is inter-
preted as the acceptance rate of positive samples
from Gclaim. Following common practice in prior
work, ACC is computed with the default thresh-
old 0 .5 on the prediction scores of the evaluated
methods, without threshold selection. This is not
necessarily optimal, since verification performance
may vary substantially with the operating thresh-
old and application requirements. More principled
alternatives include selecting the threshold on a
validation set, reporting the equal-error-rate oper-
ating point, or measuring true positive rates at
fixed false positive rates. Nevertheless, we report
default-threshold ACC for consistency with the
literature, while relying primarily on AUROC for
threshold-independent assessment.
Although same-source verification can be eval-
uated using the same family of binary verification
metrics, we do not treat it as a separate evalua-
tion setting in this section, since our experiments
mainly focus on source verification.
8.2 Datasets and Experimental
Protocol
8.2.1 Datasets
In our experiments, we considered two datasets,
OSMA Yang et al. (2023) and Liu et al. (2024)
(hereafter referred to as LiuBench), due to their
scale and diversity of synthetic sources. OSMA
focuses on GAN-generated images, whereas
LiuBench covers a broader range of generative
paradigms, including both GAN- and diffusion-
based models.
Following most existing synthetic image attri-
bution datasets and representative identification
methods, we retain real images in the experi-
mental evaluation because they are commonly
considered together with synthetic sources in prac-
tical attribution benchmarks. We also include real
images in the verification experiments, although
some prior verification studies do not explicitly
consider them. This is because, for any claimed
generator, real images are unambiguously negative
samples: they are not produced by the claimed
source and should therefore be rejected by a reli-
able verifier. Including real images in verification
allows us to assess whether a method incorrectly
accepts non-synthetic images as outputs of the
claimed generator.
However, real images are not considered as
elements of the generator set G or the known-
source set Gk defined in the previous sections,
nor are they treated as model- or architecture-
level attribution targets. Accordingly, follow-
ing the organization of OSMA, we divide the
data into four subsets: dataset-matched real
images (Matched Real ), known-source synthetic
images (Known Synthetic ), dataset-mismatched

architecture-level attribution protocol. A strict
architecture-level evaluation would require multi-

generalization can be tested across variations in
training data, random seed, hyperparameters, or
fine-tuning while preserving the same architec-
tural label.
8.2.2 Attribution evaluation
For all methods, training or adaptation, when
required, is performed only on images from the
Matched Real and Known Synthetic subsets.
In closed-set identification evaluations, the
label space consists of the real category and the
synthetic sources in the Known Synthetic subset.
Test samples are drawn from the Matched Real,
Known Synthetic , and Mismatched Real subsets,
while Unknown Synthetic samples are excluded.
Since Mismatched Real images do not corre-
spond to generative models, they do not introduce
additional attribution sources. Instead, they are
included to evaluate whether the real class remains
recognizable under a real-image distribution shift.
In open-set identification evaluations,
Unknown Synthetic samples are additionally
included at test time. The test set, therefore,
contains Matched Real , Known Synthetic , Mis-
matched Real , and Unknown Synthetic . This
setting assesses whether a method can recognize
Matched Real and Mismatched Real images as
real, attribute Known Synthetic images to the
correct known sources, and reject Unknown Syn-
thetic images as unseen synthetic sources, rather
than assigning them to any known synthetic
source or to the real class. Accordingly, open-set
results are reported only for methods equipped
with a rejection mechanism.
For verification experiments, real images are
also included as negative samples. Given a claimed
generator, positive samples are images gener-
ated by that generator, whereas negative samples
include images generated by other generators as
well as real images. This setting reflects the fact

8.2.3 Robustness evaluation
To assess robustness against common image per-
turbations, we evaluated the methods under three
image-level operations: JPEG compression, resiz-
ing, and Gaussian blurring. The parameter set-
tings are reported in Table 5. For each operation,
we include the original images as a control point,
denoted by QF = 100 for JPEG compression,
r = 1.00 for resizing, and ρ = 0 for Gaussian blur-
ring. JPEG compression is applied by re-encoding
the image with the specified quality factor when
QF < 100. Gaussian blurring is applied with blur
radius ρ when ρ > 0. For resizing, each image
is first resized according to the specified scaling
ratio r when r ̸= 1, and then passed through the
original pre-processing pipeline of the correspond-
ing method, which remains unchanged across all
robustness settings.
Robustness may also depend on the spe-
cific training strategies. Several methods explic-
itly include robustness- or generalization-oriented
components, such as perturbation-aware train-
ing Bui et al. (2022) and image augmentations
Abady et al. (2024). These strategies can improve
robustness against common operators such as
compression, resizing, blurring, or related distri-
bution shifts. In our experiments, we did not
introduce additional robustness modules or har-
monize the training pipelines across methods.
Instead, each baseline was evaluated following its
official implementation and released training pro-
tocol. Therefore, the reported robustness results
should be interpreted as the behavior of the orig-
inal method designs under a common test-time
perturbation protocol.

8.3.3 Robustness Analysis
The robustness of the identification methods is
reported in Fig. 8 and Fig. 9. Fig. 8 reports closed-
set accuracy under JPEG compression, resizing,
and blurring on LiuBench and OSMA, while Fig. 9
reports open-set AUROC and OSCR for the meth-
ods equipped with rejection mechanisms. For each
operation, the control point corresponds to the
original, unprocessed images: JPEG quality factor
100 denotes no JPEG recompression, resize ratio
r = 1 .0 denotes no resizing, and blur radius 0
denotes no blurring.
Overall, common image-processing operations
have a significant impact on source identification,
but the effect depends strongly on the dataset,
the attribution method, and the type of pertur-
bation. JPEG compression and blurring generally
reduce performance as the perturbation becomes
stronger, because they suppress or distort resid-
ual, spectral, and texture-level traces that may
carry source information. Resizing behaves differ-
ently: its unperturbed point is located at r =
1.0, and performance often decreases when images
are scaled either down or up, since interpola-
tion changes both spatial statistics and frequency
distributions. Therefore, the robustness curves
should be interpreted as operation-specific sensi-
tivity analyses, rather than as a single monotonic
robustness ranking.
Closed-set robustness
In the closed-set case, the robustness behavior is
clearly different on LiuBench and OSMA.
On LiuBench, several supervised methods
remain relatively stable under JPEG compression.

Blurring is one of the most damaging pertur-
bations, especially on OSMA. As the blur radius
increases, the accuracy of most methods decreases
substantially. This is expected because blurring
directly removes fine-grained local structures and
high-frequency components, which are important
for many direct attribution methods. RepMix
again shows the strongest closed-set robustness
under blurring, particularly on OSMA, although

stronger. On LiuBench, RepMix remains the most
stable under strong blurring, while DNA-Det, DE-

blur is highly dependent on whether the learned
attribution cues are carried by fine-scale artifacts
or by more stable higher-level representations.
The reference-based MSFA variants should be
interpreted separately from the other methods.
On LiuBench, both MSFA variants start from
much lower clean accuracy and remain far below
the other methods under all perturbations. On
OSMA, they are also highly sensitive to JPEG
compression, resizing, and blurring. This suggests
that, under the present protocol, gallery-based fin-
gerprint matching is sensitive to post-processing
that may shift the query features away from the
gallery fingerprints. The difficulty is further ampli-
fied on LiuBench, where the label space includes
heterogeneous real and synthetic sources rather
than only compact synthetic generator classes.
Taken together, the closed-set robustness
results show that high clean-setting accuracy
does not necessarily imply robustness to post-
processing. RepMix provides the most sta-
ble behavior across perturbations, especially on
OSMA, which is consistent with its design for
robustness to benign transformations. DNA-Det
and UA are highly competitive under clean
and mildly perturbed conditions on LiuBench,
but their robustness is more dataset- and
perturbation-dependent. MSFA remains limited in
this closed-set protocol, especially when the candi-
date set contains heterogeneous real and synthetic
categories.
Open-set robustness
For open-set robustness, the analysis is restricted
to POSE and UA. The results show a clear
dataset-dependent trend. On LiuBench, UA con-
sistently outperforms POSE in both AUROC and
OSCR under most JPEG compression, resizing,
and blurring conditions. This is consistent with
the clean open-set results, where UA is better
suited to the mixed GAN- and diffusion-based
composition of LiuBench. Its CLIP-based inter-
mediate features appear to provide a more stable
separation between known and unknown synthetic
sources under common image processing opera-
tions.
On OSMA, the comparison is more balanced
and, in several cases, POSE is more competitive,
especially in OSCR. Under JPEG compression,
POSE maintains higher OSCR than UA across
most quality factors, although both methods
degrade as compression becomes stronger. Under
resizing, both methods perform best around the
original scale r = 1.0 and decline when the scale
is changed, confirming that open-set rejection is
also sensitive to interpolation and scale mismatch.
Under blurring, both AUROC and OSCR decrease
as the blur radius increases, with OSCR showing a
particularly strong drop. This indicates that blur-
ring affects not only known-class classification, but
also the separability between known and unknown
sources.
The different behavior of POSE and UA
reflects their different assumptions. POSE is
designed for GAN-centric open-set model attribu-
tion and explicitly simulates open-space samples
by modifying fingerprints of known sources. This
makes it more suitable for OSMA-like settings,
where unknown sources are organized around vari-
ations in seed, architecture, or training dataset.
UA, instead, relies on intermediate features from a
large pre-trained vision model, which are less tied
to a specific GAN fingerprint model and there-
fore transfer better to LiuBench, where the known
and unknown sources span more heterogeneous
generative paradigms.

that rejection performance is sensitive to common
post-processing operations. This is particularly
evident when considering OSCR, because post-

8.4.1 Baseline Implementation
We evaluated two verification-based attribu-
tion methods on LiuBench Liu et al. (2024),
OCC-CLIP15 Liu et al. (2024), and Abady et
al.16 Abady et al. (2024). We used the offi-
cial implementations and adapted only the data
construction to our unified source verification pro-
tocol. Each known synthetic generator, namely
SD, VQ-D, LDM, ProGAN, and StyleGAN2, was
taken in turn as the claimed generator Gclaim.
For testing on each claimed generator, we used
1000 images for each real-image source, namely
MSCOCO, ImageNet, Flickr, and CC12M. For
known synthetic sources, we used 1000 images for
SD, VQ-D, and LDM, and 800 images for ProGAN
and StyleGAN2. For unknown synthetic sources,
we used 1000 images for GLIDE and GALIP, and
500 images for GauGAN.
For OCC-CLIP, we kept its official source-
specific binary verification pipeline. For each
Gclaim, images from the claimed generator were
15https://github.com/uwFengyuan/OCC-CLIP
16https://github.com/lydialy8/openset attribution
used as positives, while all known non-claimed
sources, including real-image sources and the
remaining known generators, were used as nega-
tives. For each non-claimed source, we randomly
sampled 100 negative images, and sampled the
same number of positive images from Gclaim to
ensure class balance. We used the official Ope-
nAI CLIP ViT-B/16 backbone, kept the CLIP
encoders frozen, and optimized only the learnable
prompts.
For Abady et al. (2024), we trained a single
model and used it to verify all claimed generators.
The embedding network was trained on matched
real and known synthetic images to learn source-
discriminative representations. The Siamese deci-
sion network was then trained on top of the
frozen embedding network with balanced positive
and negative pairs, as in Eq. (6). At test time,
we followed the one-vs-many claim-based protocol
of Abady et al. (2024): for each claimed genera-
tor, 100 reference images were randomly selected,
and the minimum pairwise score over the refer-
ence set was used as the final verification score. For
ROC-based metrics, we used the negative Siamese
score so that larger values indicate stronger source
consistency.
Note that the original methods above focus on
synthetic images and do not include pristine real
images as input. We included real images to align
the methods with the unified real-image treatment
adopted in this section.
We first report closed-set and open-set veri-
fication results under the standard, unprocessed
setting in Section 8.4.2. We then analyze robust-
ness under common image processing operations
in Section 8.4.3.

8.4.2 Verification Results
Tables 9, 10, and 11 report source verification
results for non-processed images. For each claimed
generator Gclaim, verification is evaluated inde-
pendently. In each source-specific column, images
from Gclaim are used as positive samples, while
images from the evaluated source subset are
used as negative samples. Therefore, a column
corresponding to a particular source should be
interpreted as the binary verification performance
between Gclaim and that source, rather than as a
metric computed only on images from that source.
In the closed-set case, the “All” column pools all
non-claimed closed-set sources as negatives. In the
open-set case, the “All” column pools all unknown
synthetic sources as negatives. AUROC reflects
threshold-independent separability, whereas ACC
is computed at the default threshold 0.5 and there-
fore also reflects the calibration of the verification
score.
Closed-set performance
Tables 9 and 10 show that closed-set verifica-
tion is generally effective for both methods. In
terms of AUROC, OCC-CLIP achieves high over-
all values for all claimed generators, ranging from
98.51% for SD to 99 .92% for StyleGAN2. Abady
et al. (2024) reaches nearly saturated AUROC val-
ues, with all overall results close to or equal to
100%. These results indicate that, when the nega-
tive sources are drawn from the known closed-set
categories, both methods can separate claimed-
source images from non-claimed images with high
ranking quality.
The ACC results provide a more threshold-
dependent view. Abady et al. (2024) remains
highly stable at the default threshold, with overall
ACC values from 99.77% to 100.00%. OCC-CLIP
also performs well, but its overall ACC varies more
clearly across claimed generators, from 91.09% for
SD to 98 .02% for StyleGAN2. This gap between
AUROC and ACC suggests that OCC-CLIP often
ranks positive and negative samples correctly, but
its default threshold is not equally well calibrated
for all claimed sources.
40
The source-specific entries reveal several diffi-
cult verification pairs. For OCC-CLIP, SD claims
are more affected by CC12M real images, where
AUROC and ACC drop to 92 .06% and 79 .30%,
respectively. The VQ-D and LDM pair is also
challenging: when VQ-D is claimed, and LDM
is used as negatives, OCC-CLIP obtains 93 .81%
AUROC and 78.20% ACC; when LDM is claimed,
and VQ-D is used as negatives, it obtains 95 .21%
AUROC and 85 .25% ACC. This suggests that
related diffusion-based sources may occupy nearby
regions in the verification space, making fixed-
threshold decisions more difficult. By contrast,
Abady et al. (2024) remains almost saturated
across these closed-set source pairs, indicating
strong separation under this evaluation protocol.
The matched-source entries in Table 10 cor-
respond to the acceptance rate of positive sam-
ples from Gclaim. Both methods generally accept
positive samples reliably. OCC-CLIP shows high
acceptance rates for all claimed generators,
although LDM is slightly lower than the oth-
ers. Abady et al. (2024) remains close to sat-
uration. Overall, the closed-set results indicate
that source verification is reliable when negatives
come from the known evaluation source set, but
fixed-threshold performance can still vary across
claimed generators and source pairs.
Open-set performance
Table 11 reports verification against unknown
synthetic sources. Compared with the closed-set
case, the results show stronger dependence on the
claimed generator and on the unknown negative
source. This is expected because the verifier must
reject images from sources that are not part of the
closed-set training configuration.
For SD and StyleGAN2 claims, both methods
maintain very high open-set performance. OCC-
CLIP obtains 99 .98%/99.31% AUROC/ACC for
SD and 99 .97%/98.79% for StyleGAN2 in the
“All” column. Abady et al. (2024) obtains sim-
ilarly high results, with 100 .00%/99.71% for SD
and 99.96%/98.61% for StyleGAN2. These results
suggest that, for these claimed generators, the
unknown synthetic sources remain well separated
from the claimed-source images.
The open-set setting becomes more difficult
for VQ-D and LDM claims. For OCC-CLIP, the
overall AUROC/ACC drops to 88 .39%/56.54%
for VQ-D and 79 .33%/54.37% for LDM. The
most difficult negative sources are GLIDE and
GALIP, especially for LDM, where OCC-CLIP
obtains only 80.94% AUROC against GLIDE and
67.39% AUROC against GALIP. Abady et al.
(2024) shows higher AUROC values for these
cases, reaching 99 .73% for VQ-D and 93 .95% for
LDM in the “All” column. However, its ACC
remains much lower for LDM, with only 59 .40%
in the “All” column. This indicates that high
ranking separability does not necessarily translate
into reliable decisions at the default threshold,
especially for challenging diffusion-related claims.
For ProGAN claims, both methods perform
well against GLIDE and GALIP but show a
clear drop against GauGAN. OCC-CLIP obtains
92.22% AUROC and 74 .08% ACC against Gau-
GAN, while Abady et al. (2024) obtains 84 .66%
AUROC and 69 .46% ACC. Since both ProGAN
and GauGAN are GAN-based sources, this pair
may be harder to separate under the learned
verification space than cross-family comparisons
against diffusion-based or text-to-image sources.
This result suggests that open-set verification dif-
ficulty is not determined only by whether the
negative source is unseen, but also by its proximity
to the claimed generator in the learned verification
space.
Overall, verification-based attribution is effec-
tive under closed-set conditions, and both the
methods we have tested can achieve high
threshold-independent separability in many open-
set cases. However, the open-set results also
show two limitations. First, performance depends
strongly on the claimed generator and the
unknown negative source, with VQ-D, LDM, and
ProGAN–GauGAN pairs being more challenging.
Second, ACC at the default threshold can be much
lower than one could expect by looking at the
AUROC, showing that score calibration is critical
for practical source verification.

used for verification, but the degree of degradation

perturbation-specific trends, but the curves are
more variable because the negative samples come

SD and StyleGAN2 claims. For OCC-CLIP, VQ-

open-set results, where these claims are more dif-
ficult. ProGAN is relatively stable under resizing
but shows noticeable degradation under JPEG
compression and blurring. For Abady et al. (2024),
ProGAN is particularly sensitive to JPEG com-
pression, while SD and StyleGAN2 remain more
stable. These observations indicate that compres-
sion robustness in open-set verification depends on
both the claimed generator and the distribution
of unknown negative sources.
Once again, for resizing, both methods perform
best or near best around the original scale. Strong
downsampling is more harmful than upsampling,
especially for VQ-D and LDM claims. StyleGAN2
remains stable across almost all resize ratios, and
SD remains relatively robust around the origi-
nal scale and under upsampling, although it can
degrade under strong downsampling. These results
are consistent with the closed-set observations and
indicate that scale changes can shift verification
scores, particularly when the processed images
deviate substantially from the original resolution.
Blurring remains challenging in the open-
set case. OCC-CLIP preserves high AUROC for
SD and StyleGAN2, but VQ-D, LDM, and Pro-
GAN show clear degradation as the blur radius
increases. For Abady et al. (2024), SD and Style-
GAN2 are again relatively stable under mild
blur, whereas VQ-D, LDM, and ProGAN degrade
more substantially. These drops suggest that blur
can reduce the separability between the claimed
generator and unknown synthetic sources, espe-
cially for claims whose verification cues are more
affected by the suppression of local texture and
high-frequency information.
Method-level observations
The two verification methods exhibit different
robustness patterns. OCC-CLIP, as a claim-
specific few-shot verifier built on CLIP features,
remains stable for some claims, such as SD and
StyleGAN2, but is more sensitive for VQ-D and
LDM, especially in open-set verification. This is
consistent with the clean-setting results, where
VQ-D and LDM are also more challenging claimed
generators. The Siamese verifier in Abady et al.
(2024) is generally stable under resizing and under
JPEG compression for several claims. This behav-
ior is in line with its Siamese embedding learning
43
and the use of training augmentations, includ-
ing JPEG compression and color transformations,
although the plots do not isolate the effect of
any single augmentation. Its robustness is nev-
ertheless not uniform: ProGAN is sensitive to
compression, and VQ-D, LDM, or ProGAN can
degrade substantially under blurring depending on
the setting.
Comparing closed-set and open-set results, the
main conclusion is that verification robustness
is highly claim-dependent. The same operation
can have a limited impact for SD or StyleGAN2,
while causing substantial degradation for VQ-D,
LDM, or ProGAN. Therefore, verification robust-
ness should not be summarized by a single global
ranking of methods or perturbations. Instead, it
depends on the interaction among the claimed
generator, the negative source distribution, the
post-processing operation, and the verification
mechanism.
Taken together, these results show that
verification-based attribution remains reliable
under mild post-processing for several claimed
generators, but it is not uniformly robust. Resiz-
ing mainly affects performance under strong
downsampling, JPEG compression is harmful for
selected claims such as ProGAN, and blurring
often causes large degradation because it sup-
presses fine-grained source traces. This confirms
that robustness remains an important issue also
for verification-based attribution, particularly in
open-set scenarios where the verifier must main-
tain separation from unseen synthetic sources
after post-processing.
9 Concluding Remarks
In this survey, we have reviewed passive post-
hoc synthetic image attribution (SIA), focusing
on methods that infer the origin of a synthetic
image from the image itself without relying on
embedded watermarks or generation-time side
information. We first formalized the main attribu-
tion tasks, including source identification, source
verification, and same-source verification, and dis-
cussed their closed-set and open-set variants. We
then analyzed the methodological design space of
SIA in terms of task formulation, set configura-
tion, attribution granularity, prior knowledge, and
decision rules. Based on this design space, we orga-
nized representative methods into direct attribu-
tion and reference-based attribution approaches,
and further discussed how different assumptions
on generator access, reference construction, and
scoring mechanisms affect their applicability. We
also reviewed representative datasets, evaluation
protocols, and empirical trends, highlighting the
evolution of SIA from controlled GAN-centric
settings toward more realistic scenarios involv-
ing diffusion-based and text-to-image generators,
limited reference data, post-processing, dataset
mismatch, and unknown sources.
Despite substantial progress, SIA remains far
from a solved problem. Existing methods often
achieve strong performance under controlled con-
ditions, but their reliability may degrade when the
source space expands, when test images are post-
processed, when semantic content shifts, or when
the queried source has not been observed dur-
ing training. These limitations indicate that future
research should move beyond improving closed-set
benchmark accuracy and focus more directly on
generalization, robustness, scalability, calibration,
and attribution granularity. Below, we summa-
rize several key challenges and future research
directions.
Bias-Free Datasets and Granularity-Aware
Benchmarks. The reliability of attribution
methods is closely tied to the quality and structure
of the datasets and benchmarks used for training
and evaluation. Existing datasets may suffer from
content imbalance, limited generator diversity,
unclear source-label definitions, or mismatched
training and testing distributions, which can lead
to biased conclusions about attribution perfor-
mance. Future datasets should provide clearer
source annotations and richer metadata about
generator versions, prompts, training data, check-
points, and post-processing operations. Future
benchmarks should further define standardized
closed-set and open-set splits, explicit verification
protocols, robustness protocols, and granularity-
aware evaluation settings. For model-level attri-
bution, benchmarks should include closely related
model instances, whereas for architecture-level
attribution, they should include multiple inde-
pendently trained or fine-tuned instances within
each architecture. Such a dataset and benchmark
44
design is essential for assessing whether SIA meth-
ods capture reliable source-related traces rather
than exploiting dataset-specific shortcuts.
Granularity-Aware Attribution. As genera-
tive models increasingly share architectural com-
ponents, training objectives, data sources, and
generation pipelines, the differences between gen-
erative sources may become subtle and highly
localized. For model-level attribution, methods
must discriminate among closely related instances,
including models differing only in random seeds,
checkpoints, fine-tuning data, or minor architec-
tural modifications. For architecture-level attribu-
tion, methods face a different but equally impor-
tant challenge: they should remain invariant to
instance-level variations within the same archi-
tecture, while still being discriminative across
different architectures. This requires representa-
tions that suppress semantic shortcuts and irrele-
vant instance-specific noise while preserving stable
source-related traces at the target granularity.
Reliable Attribution in Open-Set Condi-
tions. In practical scenarios, attribution systems
inevitably encounter images produced by sources
that are not represented during training. Open-
set SIA, therefore, cannot be reduced to closed-set
classification over a fixed set of known genera-
tors. Future work should develop principled open-
set formulations with calibrated confidence esti-
mation, robust rejection mechanisms, and task-
specific evaluation protocols. In this context,
openness may concern unseen model instances or
unseen architectures, and the core challenge is to
avoid forcing unknown-source images into one of
the known source categories.
Robustness and Cross-Distribution Gen-
eralization. Synthetic images distributed in
the wild are often subject to JPEG compres-
sion, resizing, cropping, filtering, re-encoding, or
platform-specific processing pipelines. Such post-
processing operations may attenuate or distort
source-related traces, especially when attribu-
tion relies on fragile low-level artifacts. Beyond
input-level transformations, SIA methods must
also generalize across shifts in semantic content,
image sources, prompt distributions, generator
versions, and generation pipelines. These shifts
may expose dataset-specific shortcuts and make
it difficult to determine whether a method cap-
tures stable source-related traces. Future research
should therefore develop transformation-invariant
and content-invariant representations, disentangle
semantic content from generation-related traces,
and establish standardized protocols for evalu-
ating both post-processing robustness and cross-
distribution generalization.
Scalable and Data-Efficient Attribu-
tion. The number of generative models,
fine-tuned variants, and commercial systems is
rapidly increasing, while large labeled datasets
may not be available for every newly released
or privately deployed generator. Practical SIA
systems should therefore be both scalable and
data-efficient: they should incorporate new
sources with limited samples and computation,
while preserving performance on previously
known sources. This setting is related to dynamic
or incremental attribution, where the single-query
attribution decision remains static at each time
step, while the available set of sources evolves.
Future research should investigate incremen-
tal learning, prototype-based representations,
modular reference databases, retrieval-based
attribution, and universal embedding spaces that
support rapid adaptation to novel generators. In
this context, reference-based and metric-learning
approaches are particularly promising, but their
performance depends critically on the quality,
representativeness, and construction of the avail-
able reference set. Scalability should therefore
account for computation, annotation, storage,
and long-term maintenance costs.
